# Chapter 16: Obstetric Conditions

## 16.1 Antenatal Care (ANC)

ICD-10 CODE: Z36

Antenatal care is planned medical care provided to pregnant women by a skilled birth attendant from the time of conception to delivery, aimed at ensuring a safe and satisfying pregnancy and birth outcome.

The main objective of antenatal care is to give information on:

- Screening, prevention, and treatment of complications
- Emergency preparedness
- Birth planning
- Satisfying any unmet nutritional, social, emotional, and physical needs of the pregnant woman
- Provision of patient education, including successful care and nutrition of the newborn
- Identification of high-risk pregnancy
- Encouragement of male partner involvement in antenatal care

### 16.1.1 Goal-Oriented Antenatal Care Protocol

Important: goals are different depending on the timing of the visit.

Four visits are aimed for in an uncomplicated pregnancy. If a woman books later than the first trimester, preceding goals should be combined and attended to.

At all visits:

- Address any identified problems.
- Check blood pressure.
- Measure symphysio-fundal height.
- Ensure all women receive haemoglobin testing.
- Ensure all women receive HIV testing.
- Ensure all women receive syphilis testing using RPR.

### 16.1.3 High Risk Pregnancy (HRP)

ICD-10 CODE: O09

High-risk pregnancy is a pregnancy with a higher than average risk of an adverse outcome for the mother or baby, for example abortion, intrauterine death, stillbirth, prematurity, other morbidity, or mortality.

**High-risk criteria**

A pregnancy is high risk if the woman has a history of or current:

- Extremes of reproductive age: below 18 years or above 35 years
- Primigravida, especially if too young, below 18 years, short, below 150 cm, or older than 35 years
- High parity, 5 or more, or short birth-to-pregnancy interval below 2 years
- Maternal obesity, BMI above 30
- History of large infants, 4 kg and above
- History of prematurity or low birth weight below 2.5 kg
- History of obstructed or difficult labour
- History of instrumental delivery
- Poor obstetric history, for example stillbirths, neonatal deaths, abortions, or caesarean section
- History of reproductive tract surgery, for example VVF repair, repaired ruptured uterus, surgery on the cervix, or myomectomy
- Genetic or familial diseases, such as sickle cell disease
- Medical conditions, including diabetes, HIV, cardiac disease, renal disease, hypertension, rhesus problems, or disability
- Obstetric conditions, for example multiple pregnancy, malpresentation, antepartum haemorrhage, postpartum haemorrhage, DVT, intrauterine growth restriction or foetal growth restriction, IVF pregnancy, PROM, post-dates, cephalopelvic disproportion, or surrogate pregnancy

**Management principles**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Early identification of high-risk pregnant women and referral as appropriate.<br><br>Provide preconception care and folic acid supplementation.<br><br>Provide prophylaxis and antenatal counselling to prevent some high-risk pregnancies.<br><br>Ensure early start of antenatal care.<br><br>Provide close medical supervision during pregnancy.<br><br>Conduct special investigations to evaluate foetal development and maternal well-being.<br><br>Prepare a birth preparedness plan.<br><br>Ensure timely intervention for therapy and delivery.<br><br>Ensure skilled birth attendance.<br><br>Refer early to higher level as appropriate. | HC3/HC4 |

> **Note**
> Skilled attendance at birth remains the most important component of comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care.
>
## 16.2 Management of Selected Conditions in Pregnancy

### 16.2.1 Anaemia in Pregnancy

ICD-10 CODE: O99.019

Anaemia is the most frequent and major complication of pregnancy. It may be defined as haemoglobin level below normal, that is below 11 g/dL for pregnant women. In the second trimester, the cut-off is 10.5 g/dL.

**Causes**

Causes include:

- Nutritional causes, including iron deficiency and folic acid deficiency
- Infections and infestations, including hookworm infestation, malaria, UTI, and HIV/AIDS
- Haemorrhagic causes, including bleeding in pregnancy and trauma
- Haemoglobinopathies, for example sickle cell anaemia and thalassaemias
- Malignancies
- Medicines used for HIV or cancer treatment
- Other causes

**Clinical features**

The mother may give a history of:

- Gradual onset of exhaustion or weakness
- Swelling of the legs
- Dyspnoea
- Dizziness
- Palpitations

On examination, findings may include:

- Pallor of conjunctiva, tongue, palm, vagina, or other sites, varying with severity of anaemia
- Glossitis
- Stomatitis
- Oedema of the legs
- In very severe cases, evidence of heart failure, such as engorged neck veins, dyspnoea, hepatomegaly, ascites, gallop rhythm, and oedema

**Complications**

Untreated anaemia may increase the risk of:

- Premature labour
- Poor intrauterine foetal growth
- Weak uterine contractions
- Foetal hypoxia
- Postpartum haemorrhage
- Poor lactation
- Postpartum sepsis

**Investigations**

Investigations include:

- Blood tests:
  - Haemoglobin level; below 11 g/dL is considered abnormal
  - Peripheral smear to determine the type of anaemia and presence of malaria parasites
  - Haemoglobin electrophoresis
- Stool examination for ova and cysts of hookworm infestation

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| **Prophylaxis**<br><br>All pregnant women should receive ferrous sulphate and folic acid daily from 12 weeks.<br><br>Continue supplementation until 6 months after delivery. | HC2 |
| **Severe anaemia or heart failure**<br><br>If haemoglobin is ≤7 g/dL or the patient has heart failure, refer to a well-equipped facility for further management. | HC4 |
| **Haemoglobin above 7 g/dL**<br><br>Give combination of ferrous sulphate and folic acid once daily, Fe 200 mg plus folic acid 400 micrograms.<br><br>Review the mother every 2 weeks. Haemoglobin should rise by 0.7 to 1 g/dL per week. | HC2 |
| Emphasise a realistic balanced diet rich in proteins, iron, and vitamins, for example beans, peas, millet, sorghum, peanuts, red meat, liver, dark green vegetables, fortified foods, and bananas.<br><br>Treat malaria presumptively with SP and follow up.<br><br>Deworm the patient with mebendazole 500 mg single dose in the second and third trimesters.<br><br>Treat any other cause found from investigations.<br><br>Advise child spacing with an interval of at least 2 years.<br><br>If not improving, refer to hospital. | HC2 |
| **Anaemia at 36 weeks or at delivery**<br><br>If the mother is still anaemic at 36 weeks gestation or at the time of delivery, refer to a well-equipped facility for further management, including blood transfusion. | HC4 |
| **Sickle cell disease**<br><br>If the patient has sickle cell disease, refer to a higher level for antenatal care and delivery. | HC4 |

**Prevention, health education, and mother self-care**

- Explain the possible causes of anaemia.
- Advise on nutrition and diet. The mother should increase consumption of foods rich in iron and vitamins.
- Instruct the patient to use medicines as prescribed and explain the dangers of non-compliance.
- Advise on side effects of iron medicines, for example darkened stools.
- Instruct the patient to come every 2 weeks for follow-up.

### 16.2.2 Pregnancy and HIV Infection

All HIV services for pregnant mothers are offered in the MCH clinic.

After delivery, mother and baby will remain in the MCH postnatal clinic until the HIV status of the child is confirmed. They will then be transferred to the general ART clinic.

All pregnant mothers and partners should receive routine counselling and testing for HIV.

If the mother tests negative:

- Counsel on HIV prevention.
- Repeat HIV test in the third trimester or during labour and delivery.

If the mother tests positive or is already known positive but not yet on ART:

- Enrol on HIV care for eMTCT.

If the mother is already HIV-positive and already on ART:

- Continue the existing regimen.
- She may not need to be switched to Option B+ regimens.
- Perform viral load at first contact.

#### 16.2.2.2 Counselling for HIV-Positive Mothers

Provide counselling and psychosocial support to HIV-positive mothers.

**Psychosocial support and nutrition**

- Give psychosocial support.
- Encourage mothers to enrol in Family Support Groups for peer support.
- Advise on the importance of good nutrition.
- Talk to family members to encourage the woman to eat enough and help her avoid hard physical work.
- Provide micronutrient supplementation during pregnancy and breastfeeding, including iron, folic acid, and multivitamins.
- Advise her that she is more liable to infections and should seek medical help as soon as possible.

**Birth plan**

Review the birth plan and advise her:

- To continue attending antenatal care.
- To deliver in a health facility where appropriate care can be provided for her and the baby.
- To go to the health facility as soon as labour starts or membranes rupture.

**Lochia and blood-stained sanitary pads**

Advise on the infectiousness of lochia and blood-stained sanitary pads, and how to dispose of them safely according to local facilities.

**Family planning**

If not breastfeeding exclusively, advise her to use a family planning method immediately to prevent unwanted pregnancy.

**Ongoing care**

Ensure linkage of the mother-baby pair and her family for ongoing care beyond puerperium.

**Breast care if not breastfeeding**

If not breastfeeding, advise that:

- The breasts may be uncomfortable for a while.
- She should avoid expressing breast milk to remove milk, because the more milk is removed, the more it forms.
- She should support her breasts with a firm, well-fitting bra or cloth.
- She may use paracetamol for painful breasts.
- She should seek care if breasts become painful, swollen, or red, or if she feels ill or has fever.

**Counselling on infant feeding choice**

Begin infant feeding counselling before birth when the pregnant mother has been identified to be HIV-positive.

The decision on how she will feed the baby should be made before delivery. The mother should then be supported to implement the feeding option she has chosen.

All mothers are encouraged to breastfeed their babies exclusively for 6 months and then introduce complementary feeding until 1 year.

The mother should continue ARVs throughout breastfeeding.

The child should continue cotrimoxazole prophylaxis until HIV status is confirmed negative with a PCR test at 6 weeks after stopping breastfeeding.

During the postpartum period, if a mother chooses replacement feeding from the beginning, the choice of replacement feeds should fulfil the AFASS criteria:

- Affordable
- Feasible
- Available
- Sustainable
- Safe

### 16.2.3 Chronic Hypertension in Pregnancy

ICD-10 CODE: O10, O13

Chronic hypertension in pregnancy is blood pressure above 140/90 mmHg that is present before pregnancy or starts before 20 weeks gestation.

Pregnant women with chronic hypertension should continue lifestyle modifications for controlling hypertension, including:

- No alcohol.
- Regular moderate exercise, such as brisk walking for 30 minutes at least 3 times a week.
- Smoking cessation.

The health worker should:

- Ask the mother about foetal movements at each visit.
- Aim for blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg.
- Consider labour if blood pressure is persistently ≥160/90 mmHg, pregnancy is ≥37 weeks gestation, or there is maternal or foetal compromise, for example poor symphysio-fundal height growth.

### 16.2.4 Malaria in Pregnancy

ICD-10 CODE: B54

Malaria can contribute to pregnancy complications such as:

- Abortion
- Poor foetal mental development
- Premature labour
- Intrauterine growth retardation
- Foetal death
- Severe maternal anaemia due to haemolysis
- Maternal death

Complications are more common in:

- Mothers of low gravidity, especially primigravidae and secundigravidae
- HIV-positive mothers
- Adolescents
- Women with sickle-cell disease
- Women from areas of low malaria endemicity, for example Kisoro and Kabale

See section 2.5.2 for more information on features and diagnosis of malaria.

**Management of malaria in pregnancy**

| Approach | Management | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Prophylaxis for all pregnant mothers, except those with HIV on cotrimoxazole prophylaxis | Give intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy with sulphadoxine/pyrimethamine once a month starting at 13 weeks until delivery. | HC2 |
| Treatment of uncomplicated malaria in first trimester | Give quinine oral 600 mg 8 hourly for 7 days.<br><br>If quinine is not available, ACT may be used. | HC2 |
| Treatment of uncomplicated malaria in second and third trimesters | **First line:** artemether/lumefantrine 80/480 mg 12 hourly for 3 days.<br><br>**First-line alternative:** dihydroartemisinin/piperaquine 3 tablets, 1080 mg, once daily for 3 days.<br><br>If no response, give quinine oral 600 mg 8 hourly for 7 days. | HC2/HC3/HC4 |
| Severe malaria in all trimesters and during lactation | Give IM or IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, and 24 hours, then once daily until mother can tolerate oral medication. Complete treatment with 3 days of oral ACT.<br><br>**First-line alternative:** IM artemether 3.2 mg/kg loading dose, then 1.6 mg/kg once daily until mother can tolerate oral medication. Complete treatment with 3 days of oral ACT.<br><br>If artesunate or artemether is not available, use quinine 10 mg/kg IV every 8 hours in dextrose 5%. | HC3 |
| Caution | Quinine is associated with an increased risk of hypoglycaemia in late pregnancy. | HC3 |

**Prevention and control of malaria in pregnancy**

- Use insecticide-treated mosquito nets before, during, and after pregnancy.
- Give all pregnant women intermittent preventive treatment with sulphadoxine/pyrimethamine, except where there is allergy to sulphonamides.
- Ensure prompt diagnosis and effective treatment of malaria in pregnancy.

**Education messages to mothers and the community**

- Malaria is transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes.
- Pregnant women and children are at particular risk of malaria.
- If untreated, malaria can cause severe anaemia and death in pregnant women.
- Malaria can lead to anaemia, miscarriage, stillbirth, foetal mental impairment, or low birth weight babies, who are more prone to infant and childhood mortality than normal weight babies.
- It is better and cheaper to prevent malaria than to treat it.
- The individual, family, and community can control malaria by taking appropriate actions.
- Sleeping under an insecticide-treated mosquito net is the best way to prevent malaria.
- It is important to complete the course of treatment in order to achieve cure.
- Severe complicated malaria needs special management; refer.

### 16.2.5 Diabetes in Pregnancy

ICD-10 CODE: O24

**Risk factors and indications for screening**

Screen for diabetes in pregnancy where there is:

- BMI above 35 kg/m²
- Age above 40 years
- Gestational diabetes mellitus in a previous pregnancy
- Family history of diabetes in a first-degree relative
- Previous unexplained third trimester death
- Previous macrosomic baby, weight above 4 kg
- Polyhydramnios
- Glycosuria
- Foetus large for gestational age

**Diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes**

Diagnostic criteria include:

- Fasting blood sugar above 5.6 mmol/L
- Plasma glucose above 7.8 mmol/L 2 hours after 75 g glucose tolerance test

**Therapeutic targets**

- Pre-prandial blood glucose below 5.3 mmol/L
- 1-hour postprandial glucose below 7.8 mmol/L
- 2-hour postprandial glucose below 6.4 mmol/L

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Advise smoking cessation, moderate exercise, and dietary modification. See section 19.1.3.<br><br>If obese and diabetes is mild, consider metformin 500 mg. Start with one tablet daily and increase by 500 mg per week up to a maximum of 2 g per day in divided doses. | HC3 |
| If not controlled, use insulin. See section 8.1.3. | HC4 |

## 16.3 Antenatal Complications

### 16.3.1 Hyperemesis Gravidarum

ICD-10 CODE: O21

The cause is not known, but hyperemesis gravidarum may be more common in multiple pregnancy and molar pregnancy.

**Clinical features**

- May occur from the 4th week of pregnancy and can continue beyond the 12th week.
- Defining symptoms are nausea and vomiting so severe that oral intake is compromised.
- The patient may develop complications of excessive vomiting, such as vomiting blood and dehydration.

**Differential diagnosis**

- Intestinal obstruction
- Other causes of vomiting
- Molar pregnancy

**Investigations**

- Blood:
  - Complete blood count
  - RDT for malaria parasites
- Urinalysis to exclude urinary tract infection
- Ultrasound scan to detect molar or multiple pregnancy

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Give IV fluids to correct dehydration and ketosis. See section 1.1.3.<br><br>Use Ringer’s lactate or normal saline and 5% glucose.<br><br>Give promethazine 25 mg IM or orally every 8 hours when required.<br><br>Give vitamin B6, pyridoxine, 1 tablet every 12 hours for 7 days.<br><br>Or give metoclopramide 10 mg IM, IV, or orally every 6 to 8 hours when required.<br><br>If not responding to the above, give chlorpromazine 25 mg IM or orally every 6 hours when required and refer. | HC3/HC4 |

### 16.3.2 Vaginal Bleeding in Early Pregnancy / Abortion

ICD-10 CODE: O20

Vaginal bleeding in early pregnancy is almost always abnormal, and patients may need admission or referral.

The most common causes of bleeding in the first 6 months, before 26 weeks gestation, are abortion and ectopic pregnancy.

Abortion, or miscarriage, occurs when the foetus is lost before 28 weeks of pregnancy.

**Causes**

- Not known in the majority of patients
- May be intentional, induced abortion
- May be spontaneous, often as a result of fever
- If the mother has more than 2 miscarriages, refer for assessment

**Differential diagnosis**

- Ectopic pregnancy
- Other causes of vaginal bleeding, for example cancer
- Other causes of lower abdominal pain, for example PID

**Investigations**

- Urine pregnancy test
- Ultrasound scan
- Complete blood count

**Clinical features, terminology, and management**

Clinical features and management depend on the stage of abortion.

### 16.3.3 Ectopic Pregnancy

ICD-10 CODE: O00

Ectopic pregnancy may result from partial blockage of the fallopian tube.

**Causes and predisposing factors**

- Previous infection causing partial blockage of the tube
- Congenital malformation of the fallopian tubes
- Excessively long tubes

**Risk factors**

- History of prior ectopic pregnancy
- Prior abdominal or tubal surgery
- History of PID
- Endometriosis
- History of infertility
- Cigarette smoking
- Multiple sexual partners

**Clinical features**

- Period of amenorrhoea, as in normal pregnancy
- Lower abdominal pain, often acute and followed by slight vaginal bleeding
- If the tube ruptures, the patient may suddenly become anaemic and go into shock
- Abdomen may be very tender, with rebound tenderness and guarding on palpation
- Abdomen may not move with normal breathing
- Cervical motion tenderness during vaginal examination
- Features of free fluid in the abdomen may be present

**Differential diagnosis**

- Other causes of acute abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding, for example twisted ovarian cyst
- Appendicitis
- Pelvic inflammatory disease
- Incomplete abortion

**Investigations**

Ectopic pregnancy is usually diagnosed clinically.

If the tube ruptures, there may be little time for investigations, but ultrasound can be useful if the patient is not in shock.

Investigations include:

- Pregnancy test to exclude other causes
- Complete blood count
- Blood grouping and cross-matching

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Set up IV drip with normal saline and run very slowly, just to maintain IV access.<br><br>Refer to hospital for surgery. | HC3/H |

> **Warning**
> Do not run a lot of fluids before surgery. This raises blood pressure, which may worsen bleeding and worsen the state of shock.
>
### 16.3.4 Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM and PPROM)

ICD-10 CODE: O42

PROM is rupture of membranes before the start of labour. It can occur either:

- At or after 37 weeks when the foetus is mature or term. This is PROM.
- Between 24 and 37 weeks when the foetus is immature or preterm. This is preterm PROM, or PPROM.

In all cases of PPROM, prematurity and its attendant problems are the principal concerns for the foetus, while infection morbidity and its complications are the primary concerns for the mother.

**Risk factors associated with PPROM**

- Low socioeconomic status
- Tobacco use
- Low body mass index
- Prior history of vaginal bleeding during pregnancy
- History of preterm labour
- Urinary tract infection
- Chorioamnionitis
- Cervical cerclage
- Amniocentesis

**Clinical features associated with PROM**

- Leakage of fluid or vaginal discharge
- May be with or without vaginal bleeding
- Pelvic pressure but no contractions
- If rupture of membranes has been prolonged, the patient may present with fever, abdominal pain, and foul-smelling vaginal discharge

**Investigations**

- Typical odour of amniotic fluid is diagnostic.
- Place a vaginal pad over the vulva; examine visually and by smell after 1 hour.
- Use a high-level disinfected or sterile speculum for vaginal examination; fluid may be seen coming from the cervix or forming a pool in the posterior fornix.
- Ask the patient to cough; this may cause a gush of fluid.
- If membrane rupture is not recent or leakage is gradual, confirming diagnosis may be difficult.
- Abdominal ultrasound scan may show absence of, or very low, amniotic fluid.
- If available, do nitrazine test and ferning test.

> **Warning**
> Do not do digital vaginal examination. It does not help diagnosis and may cause infection.
>
**Management of PROM after 37 weeks**

Key points:

- Over 90% of patients with PROM go into spontaneous labour within 24 hours.
- Expectant management carries a risk of infection.
- Induction of labour decreases the risk of infection without increasing caesarean section delivery rate.
- Expectant management also carries neonatal risks, including infection, abruptio placentae, foetal distress, foetal restriction deformities, and death.

**Management of PPROM before 37 weeks**

Key points:

- The primary determinant of neonatal morbidity and mortality is gestational age at delivery; therefore conservative management is needed whenever possible.
- All patients with PPROM should receive antenatal steroids for foetal lung maturity.
- All patients with PPROM should receive prophylactic antibiotics because of the high risk of infection.
- Administration of tocolytics for 48 hours may allow administration of steroids to accelerate lung maturity.

- All patients with PPROM should be cared for in a facility where a neonatal intensive care unit is available.

### 16.3.5 Chorioamnionitis

**Risk factors**

- Prolonged rupture of membranes
- Prolonged labour
- Untreated STI

**Clinical features**

- History of vaginal draining of liquor
- Fever above 37.8°C
- Maternal tachycardia
- Foetal tachycardia
- Uterine tenderness
- Foul-smelling or purulent vaginal discharge
- Acute complications, including postpartum haemorrhage, puerperal sepsis, and renal failure
- Chronic complications, including infertility due to salpingitis and/or uterine synechiae

**Investigations**

- RDT or blood slide to rule out malaria
- Urinalysis to rule out UTI
- Vaginal discharge swab for Gram stain

**Management**

Care for mother and neonate includes early delivery and antibiotic administration. The risk of neonatal sepsis is increased.

### 16.4.3 Obstructed Labour

ICD-10 CODE: O64-O66

**Causes**

- Cephalopelvic disproportion
- Large baby
- Foetal abnormalities, such as hydrocephalus or conjoined twins
- Small or deformed pelvis
- Malpresentation, where the presenting part of the foetus is not the head, for example breech, shoulder, or face presentation
- Malposition, an abnormal position of the foetal head when the head is the presenting part, for example occipito-posterior
- Any barrier that prevents the baby’s descent down the birth canal

**Clinical features**

- Strong contractions but no evidence of descent of the presenting part
- Malposition or malpresentation may be felt on abdominal examination
- In a first delivery, pains may stop spontaneously
- Foetal distress with meconium-stained liquor
- Fever and dehydration with maternal exhaustion
- In late stages, regular colicky strong pains may stop when the uterus ruptures and may be replaced by dull continuous pain
- Signs of shock if the uterus has ruptured
- Physical examination may reveal signs of shock, tender uterus, formation of Bandl’s ring, and oedematous vulva or vagina

### 16.4.4 Ruptured Uterus

ICD-10 CODE: O71.1

Ruptured uterus is partial or complete tearing of the uterus. It is common in:

- Multiparous women, meaning women who have had more than one live baby
- Women with previous caesarean section

**Causes and predisposing factors**

- Assisted deliveries or obstetric procedures
- Neglected obstructed labour
- Tearing of a poorly healed uterine scar during labour
- Short interpregnancy interval of less than 18 months after caesarean section
- Previous uterine surgery, for example myomectomy
- Damage to the uterus due to a blow, for example kick or accident
- Use of oxytocic herbs

**Clinical features**

- Cessation of regular uterine contractions or labour pains
- Continuous abdominal pain
- Vaginal bleeding
- Anxiety
- Anaemia
- Shock
- Abdomen irregular in shape
- Foetal parts easily felt under the skin if the foetus is outside the uterus
- Foetal heart not heard

**Differential diagnosis**

- Abruptio placentae
- Placenta praevia
- Other causes of acute abdomen in late pregnancy
- Ruptured spleen
- Bowel obstruction

**Investigations**

- Complete blood count
- Blood grouping and cross-matching

**Management**

Mothers with suspected ruptured uterus should be referred immediately to hospital for blood transfusion and surgical management.

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Set up IV normal saline infusion.<br><br>Give IV ceftriaxone 2 g and IV metronidazole 500 mg stat.<br><br>Refer to hospital immediately for surgical management, caesarean section with or without hysterectomy. | HC3/H |

**Prevention**

- Good antenatal care and education on early arrival to the facility for labour and delivery
- Skilled birth attendance at all deliveries
- Careful monitoring of labour using a partogram
- Minimise use of oxytocin in multiparous women
- Do not attempt fundal pressure during labour
- Do not use misoprostol for induction of labour

- Failure of the uterus to contract
- Failure of the placenta to separate, for example if it is stuck in uterine muscle, as in placenta accreta

- Ruptured uterus

- Cross-matching

### 16.4.6 Postpartum Haemorrhage (PPH)

ICD-10 CODE: O72

Postpartum haemorrhage is vaginal bleeding of more than 500 mL after vaginal delivery or more than 1,000 mL after caesarean section.

- Primary PPH occurs in the first 24 hours after delivery.
- Secondary PPH occurs between 24 hours and 6 weeks after delivery.

PPH is an emergency. It can occur in any woman and needs prompt recognition and treatment.

**Causes**

The main causes are:

- **Tone:** failure of the uterus to contract, including after precipitated labour
- **Tissue:** retained placenta, part of placenta, or membranes, which may lead to uterine atony and infection
- **Trauma:** tears or rupture involving the perineum, vagina, cervix, or uterus
- **Thrombin:** thrombotic or clotting disorders, which may occur with DIC following abruptio placentae or severe antepartum haemorrhage

**High-risk patients**

- History of previous PPH
- Multiple previous caesarean sections
- Multiple pregnancy
- Placenta praevia
- Abruptio placentae
- Precipitated labour
- Prolonged labour
- Large baby
- Hypertensive disorders

**Clinical features**

- Bleeding from the genital tract, which may be a gush of blood or a small but persistent trickle, for example more than 1 pad soaked in 5 minutes
- Uterus may still be large, soft, and not contracted, especially in primary PPH
- If the uterus is well contracted, look for tears on the perineum, vagina, cervix, or uterus
- Signs of shock may be present, including tachycardia, low blood pressure, cold clammy skin
- In secondary PPH, there may be signs of infection, such as fever or abdominal tenderness

**Investigations**

- Haemoglobin and blood group should already have been done and recorded during antenatal care. If not, do them urgently.
- Women at high risk of PPH should have blood cross-matched and at least 2 units booked.
- If time allows, for example in secondary PPH, check haemoglobin and clotting.

**Management principles**

Management has two major components:

1. Resuscitation and management of obstetric haemorrhage and possible hypovolaemic shock.
2. Identification and management of the underlying cause.

- Clamp and cut the cord 3 to 5 minutes after birth or when the cord stops pulsating.
- Use controlled cord traction during a contraction with counter-traction to deliver the placenta.
- Massage the uterus immediately after delivery of the placenta to ensure the uterus is contracted.
- Identify mothers at risk and manage accordingly.
- Give 5 days of prophylactic antibiotics in prolonged or obstructed labour, or where other risk factors are present, such as rupture of membranes, birth before arrival at a health facility, or instrument delivery.

> Carbetocin should be given as a single dose.
>

## 16.5 Essential Care of the Newborn

### 16.5.1 Newborn Resuscitation

ICD-10 CODE: P22

Start resuscitation within 1 minute of birth if the baby is not breathing or is gasping for breath.

**General principles**

- Observe universal hygiene precautions to prevent infection.
- Prepare for resuscitation at each delivery, even where there are no signs of foetal distress, in case the baby requires it.

**Minimum preparation for every birth**

Ensure that the following equipment is available and in good working order:

- Two warm cotton cloths and a small one to position the head
- Heat source to keep the baby warm
- Mucus extractor, such as a penguin sucker or bulb syringe
- Ambu bag and newborn masks of varying sizes, size 0 and size 1
- Pulse oximeter
- Clock or watch
- Birth attendant skilled in newborn resuscitation

**Management**

|---|---|
| Keep the baby warm by drying the baby using the first cotton cloth, then change to the second dry cotton cloth.<br><br>Rub the back 2 to 3 times.<br><br>Clamp and cut the cord if necessary.<br><br>Transfer the baby to a dry, clean, warm surface.<br><br>Tell the mother that the baby is having difficulty starting to breathe and that you will help the baby. | HC3 |
| Open the airway:<br><br>- Position the head so that it is slightly extended.<br>- Place a folded towel less than 2 cm thick under the baby’s shoulders.<br>- Suction only if there are secretions in the mouth or nose, or if the baby was born through meconium-stained amniotic fluid.<br>- Suction 5 cm in the mouth and 3 cm in the nose while withdrawing, for a maximum of 10 seconds in total.<br>- Do not suction too deep into the throat, because this may cause the heart to slow down or breathing to stop. | HC3 |
| If still not breathing, select an appropriate mask size to cover the chin, mouth, and nose, then ventilate:<br><br>- Form a seal with the mask covering the chin, mouth, and nose.<br>- Squeeze the bag 5 times.<br>- Observe the chest.<br><br>If the chest is not rising:<br><br>- Reposition the head.<br>- Check the mask seal.<br>- Squeeze the bag harder.<br><br>Once there is a good seal and the chest is rising, ventilate for 1 minute at 40 squeezes per minute, then stop and look for breathing. | HC3 |
| If breathing is more than 30 breaths per minute and there is no severe chest in-drawing:<br><br>- Stop ventilating.<br>- Put the baby skin-to-skin on the mother’s chest.<br>- Observe every 15 minutes for breathing and warmth.<br>- Take temperature.<br>- Count breaths.<br>- Observe for chest in-drawing or grunting respiration.<br>- Monitor SpO₂.<br>- Encourage the mother to breastfeed within 1 hour.<br>- Do not leave the baby alone. | HC3 |
| If breathing is less than 30 breaths per minute or there is severe chest in-drawing:<br><br>- Continue ventilating.<br>- Arrange immediate referral.<br>- Give oxygen if available.<br>- Reassess every 1 to 2 minutes.<br>- Continue ventilation during referral.<br><br>If the baby is not gasping or breathing at all after 20 minutes of ventilation, stop ventilation. The baby is dead. | HC3 |

**Notes**

- Room air is sufficient in the absence of oxygen.
- Cardiac massage is rarely required and is dangerous when done incorrectly.
- A slow heart rate almost always responds to good breathing assistance only.
- Usually, drugs are not needed if prompt and sufficient ventilation is provided.

**Harmful and ineffective resuscitation practices**

Avoid:

- Routine suction of the newborn’s mouth and nose as soon as the head is born
- Stimulation of the newborn by slapping or flicking the soles of the feet
- Postural drainage, such as putting the baby upside down, and slapping the back
- Squeezing the back to remove secretions from the airway
- Routine giving of sodium bicarbonate to newborns who are not breathing
- Intubation by an unskilled person

### 16.6.3 Mastitis / Breast Abscess

ICD-10 CODE: O91

Mastitis or breast abscess may occur when organisms enter through a cracked nipple into an engorged breast. Less commonly, streptococci may be involved.

**Clinical features**

- Pain in the breast
- Swollen, often shiny, tender breast with enlarged veins
- Often occurs in the second postpartum week
- Fever
- May progress to breast abscess, a collection of pus within the breast tissue
- Localised erythema, shiny red skin
- Firm lump, which may later become fluctuant
- May drain pus spontaneously

**Complications**

- Recurrent infection
- Scarring
- Loss of breast size
- Noticeable breast asymmetry
- Mammary duct fistula formation due to recurrence

**Differential diagnosis**

- Breast engorgement, for mastitis
- Breast lump or cancer, for abscess

**Investigations**

- Breast milk for culture and sensitivity
- Ultrasound scan to rule out breast abscess in patients with recurrent mastitis

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Stop breastfeeding on the affected breast, but express and discard milk to avoid breast engorgement.<br><br>Give analgesics such as paracetamol 1 g every 8 hours for 3 days.<br><br>Apply warm compresses to relieve pain in the affected breast.<br><br>Continue breastfeeding on the normal breast.<br><br>Give cloxacillin 500 mg 6 hourly for 10 days.<br><br>If cloxacillin is not available, give amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours for 10 days. | HC2/HC3/H |
| If penicillin allergy is present, give erythromycin 500 mg every 6 hours for 10 days.<br><br>Or give cephalexin 500 mg orally every 6 hours for 10 days. | HC2/HC3/H |
| If not improving, refer to hospital for ultrasound scan and further management.<br><br>If clinical or ultrasound features of breast abscess are present, incise and drain. | H |

**Prevention**

- Ensure proper attachment of baby on the breast.
- Encourage frequent emptying of the breast.
- Ensure the baby sucks on the areola and not the nipple.
- Manage breast engorgement if not breastfeeding or after loss of baby. Refer to the section on care of mother and baby immediately after delivery.

## 16.7 Intrauterine Fetal Demise (IUFD) or Fetal Death in Utero (FDIU)

Fetal demise (fetal death) refers to situations in which the fetus is no longer alive, but the uterus has not yet started to expel its contents and the cervical os remains closed. Intrauterine fetal demise or death refers to babies with no signs of life in utero. “Early IUFD” refers to fetal demise before 20 weeks’ gestation and IUFD for demise of a fetus at 20 weeks gestation through to term.

**Causes**

- Birth defects (congenital abnormalities of the fetus)
- Blood transfer from baby to mother (feto-maternal haemorrhage)
- Maternal or fetal infection
- Genetic abnormality of the fetus
- Placenta separated from the inner uterine wall (placental abruption)
- Umbilical cord issues
- Uterine rupture

**Prevention**

This should be done during antenatal and intrapartum to reduce fetal death.

- Detection and treatment of syphilis / malaria
- Detection and management of hypertensive disease of pregnancy
- Management of sickle cell disease
- Detection and management of diabetes
- Monitoring during labour

**Diagnosis**

- Vaginal bleeding
- Absence or cessation of fetal movements — the usual reason for consultation
- A uterus that is significantly smaller than the expected size i.e., fundal height too small for gestational age or decrease in fundal height from a prior visit
- Absence of fetal heart sounds on electronic auscultation
- Sometimes, breast engorgement, indicating the end of the pregnancy
- The above signs suggest fetal death but are not sufficiently sensitive to justify a hasty, rash decision. Errors are common. Repeat the exam, do not rush.
- Ultrasonography paired with indicative clinical findings is essential for the accurate diagnosis / confirmation of intrauterine fetal death (IUFD)

**Common etiologies of IUFD <28 weeks gestation**

- Infection or other medical conditions
- Placental abruption or insufficiency / intrauterine growth restriction
- Congenital malformations of the fetus
- Umbilical cord accidents or other complications

**Medical management of IUFD ≥14 to ≤28 weeks**

IUFD may be managed expectantly or treated surgically (D&E) or medically.

Discussions aim to foster shared decision making about the plan for care and support maternal / parental choice.

Supportive social and psychological care should be made available to all bereaved parents.

A combination of mifepristone and misoprostol should be the first-line intervention.


Expectant management of IUFD involves awaiting spontaneous labour and may take up to 3 weeks.

Recommendations about labour and birth should take into account the mother’s preferences, her medical condition and previous intrapartum history.

Vaginal birth is the recommended mode of delivery for most women, but caesarean birth may need to be considered in individual cases.

Pregnancy tissue should be treated in the same way as other biological material unless the individual expresses a desire for it to be managed otherwise.

If a woman has had a previous caesarean section, a discussion as to the safety and benefits of induction of labour needs to be undertaken by a consultant obstetrician.

Clinical assessment and evaluation are recommended to assess maternal wellbeing and to determine the cause of death, the chance of recurrence, and of avoiding future pregnancy complications.

Laboratory tests are recommended to rule out any maternal disease or risk factor that may have contributed to the IUFD.

Fetal karyotyping should be considered in all cases.

Parents should be offered a full postmortem examination of the baby.

Postmortem examination should include external examination with birth weight, histology of relevant tissues and plain radiography (skeletal survey).

Pathological examination of the cord, membranes and placenta is recommended in all cases of IUFD.

Standardized checklists should be used to ensure that all appropriate care options are offered and that each response mark is recorded.

A standardized dataset should be collected for all IUFDs.

All IUFDs should be reviewed in a multi-professional meeting using a standardized approach.

All term intrapartum deaths with no evidence of a major congenital anomaly should be investigated locally.

Staff working with bereaved parents should be provided with an opportunity to develop their knowledge and understanding of perinatal loss, together with the development of skills in working in this area.

A system should be in place to give clinical and psychological support to staff involved with an IUFD.

A follow-up appointment with the consultant obstetrician should be arranged and it should be clear who is responsible for making these arrangements.

Women with a history of IUFD should attend a consultant-led hospital-based antenatal clinic in their next pregnancy and undergo increased antenatal surveillance.

>
Medical management of IUFD with mifepristone and misoprostol combined is contraindicated in any person with a known allergy to either medication, ectopic pregnancy, chronic adrenal failure, or inherited porphyria, and used with caution in women with life-threatening un-stabilized conditions such as uncontrolled cardiac disease, severe anaemia, or hemorrhagic disorders, uncontrolled serious asthma or in those with an IUD in place.

### Medical Management of Induced Abortion

Medical management of induced abortion (for both viable and non-viable pregnancies) at early or later gestational ages involves the use of a single-drug regimen or a combination regimen of medicines used in sequence, with specific dosages and routes of administration.

In Uganda, the following categories of people who can get services for termination of pregnancy:

- Severe maternal illnesses threatening the health of a pregnant woman e.g. severe cardiac disease, renal disease, severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia
- Severe foetal abnormalities which are not compatible with extra-uterine life e.g. molar pregnancy, anencephaly; cancer cervix
- HIV-positive women requesting for a termination
- Rape, incest, and defilement

> **Note**
> Medical termination of pregnancy services can be provided at:
>
> - HC IV
> - General hospital
> - Referral hospital levels
>
> By a medical officer, or gynae / surgeon.
>
#### Pain Management

Should be proactive for medical management of induced abortion at any gestational age. Pain medication should be offered routinely (e.g. non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]). It should be provided for the individual to use if and when wanted. Acetominophen can be used for pain control when NSAIDS are unavailable. Other methods of pain control, such as certain anti-emetics and epidural anaesthesia can be considered as necessary, with the goal of proactive, patient-centered pain management.

> **Precaution**
> - Ectopic pregnancy should be excluded, and intra-uterine gestation confirmed before the medical abortion. The medical abortion regimen will not terminate the ectopic pregnancy.
> - Fertility can return within two weeks therefore all patients should be given post-abortion contraception where eligible.
> - Access to appropriate medical care must be assured in case an emergency develops; the patient should be given clear verbal and written instructions on whom she should contact and where to go in case of concerns or suspected complications.
>

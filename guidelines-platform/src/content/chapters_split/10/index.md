# Chapter 10: Musculoskeletal and Joint Diseases

## 10.1 Infectious Musculoskeletal Conditions

### 10.1.1 Pyogenic Arthritis (Septic Arthritis)

**ICD-10 CODE:** M00

Pyogenic arthritis, also called septic arthritis, is an acute infection of a single joint, usually a large joint, and commonly affects children.

**Causes**

- Usually haematogenous spread from a primary focus following bacteraemia, such as:
    - Septic skin lesions
    - Sinus infections
    - Throat infections
    - Abrasions
    - Wounds
    - Pressure sores
    - Osteomyelitis
- Common organisms in acute arthritis include:
    - *Staphylococcus aureus*
    - Gram-negative bacilli, such as *Salmonella* species
    - *Streptococcus* species
    - Gonococcus
- Chronic septic arthritis may be caused by:
    - *Brucella*
    - Tuberculosis

**Clinical features**

- Swollen and warm joint
- Severe pain
- Reduced or abolished movement
- Temporary loss of limb function, also called pseudoparalysis
- Localised heat and tenderness
- Systemic symptoms, including fever and general malaise
- Neonates may not show fever but may refuse to feed
- Irreversible joint damage may occur if immediate treatment is not established

**Differential diagnosis**

- Inflammatory joint disease
- Intra-articular haemorrhage, such as haemophilia and other bleeding disorders
- Trauma
- Osteomyelitis of neighbouring bone

**Investigations**

- Full blood count
- Blood culture and sensitivity
- ESR, usually elevated
- Joint fluid aspiration for culture and sensitivity
- Joint fluid Gram stain
- If aspiration fails to obtain pus, use arthrotomy in theatre

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Provide pain relief, such as paracetamol or ibuprofen. | HC2 |
| Immobilise the involved limb and try splinting. | HC2 |
| Refer urgently to HC4 or hospital. | HC2 |
| Aspirate articular fluid for Gram stain and culture and sensitivity, if available. Use local skin and subcutaneous anaesthesia if indicated. | HC4 |
| Repeat aspiration daily until no further pus is obtained. | HC4 |
| Use diazepam 2.5 mg rectally for sedation in children, if required. | HC4 |
| Alternatively, perform open drainage in theatre. | RR |
| Continue pain relief with paracetamol or ibuprofen. | HC4 |
| Alternatively, give diclofenac 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC4 |
| Child: diclofenac 0.32 mg/kg rectally every 6 to 8 hours, maximum 150 mg. | HC4 |
| Alternatively, give indomethacin 25 to 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC4 |
| Child: indomethacin 0.5 to 1 mg/kg every 12 hours. | HC4 |

**Antibiotic treatment**

If possible, antibiotic treatment should be guided by Gram stain and culture and sensitivity results.

| Situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Gram-positive organisms on Gram stain, or negative Gram stain in an immunocompetent adult patient | Give cloxacillin 500 mg to 1 g IV every 6 hours. | HC4 |
| Gram-positive organisms on Gram stain, or negative Gram stain in an immunocompetent adult patient | Child: cloxacillin 50 mg/kg IV every 6 hours. | HC4 |
| Gram-positive organisms on Gram stain, or negative Gram stain in an immunocompetent adult patient | Give IV treatment for 2 weeks, then if improving, switch to oral treatment to complete 4 weeks. | HC4 |
| Alternative or second-line treatment | Give chloramphenicol 500 mg IV every 6 hours for at least 2 weeks. | HC4 |
| Alternative or second-line treatment | Child: chloramphenicol 12.5 mg/kg every 6 hours. | HC4 |
| Gram-negative organisms on Gram stain | Give ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily for 2 to 4 weeks. | HC4 |
| Alternative for Gram-negative infection | Give ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 hours for 3 weeks. | HC4 |
| Negative Gram stain in adults with underlying conditions, such as suspected *Salmonella* in sickle cell disease, all children with negative Gram stain, or patients with underlying conditions | Give cloxacillin plus ceftriaxone. | HC4 |
| Suspected gonococcal infection, such as in sexually active adolescents | Give ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily for 1 week. | HC4 |

### 10.1.2 Osteomyelitis

**ICD-10 CODE:** M86

Osteomyelitis is infection of bone by pus-forming bacteria. It mainly affects older children and adults.

**Causes**

- Any type of bacterium, but most commonly *Staphylococcus aureus*
- Infection may follow infection elsewhere in the body
- Sickle cell disease is a risk factor
- In sickle cell disease, the causative agent is mostly *Staphylococcus aureus*, although *Salmonella* is also common

**Clinical features**

**Acute osteomyelitis**

- Onset is usually over several days
- Fever, usually high, but may be absent, especially in neonates
- Severe pain
- Tenderness and increased heat at the site of infection
- Swelling of the surrounding tissues and joint
- Reduced or complete loss of use of the affected limb
- The patient is usually a child aged 4 years or above with reduced immunity, but adults may also be affected
- A history of injury may be given and may be misleading, especially if there is no fever

**Chronic osteomyelitis**

- May present with pain, erythema, or swelling
- May be associated with a draining sinus tract
- Deep or extensive ulcers that fail to heal after several weeks of appropriate ulcer care should raise suspicion of chronic osteomyelitis
- Non-healing fractures should also raise suspicion of chronic osteomyelitis

**Differential diagnosis**

- Infection of joints
- Limb trauma or fracture, especially in children
- Bone cancer, such as osteosarcoma around the knee
- Pyomyositis
- Cellulitis
- Sickle-cell disease thrombotic crisis

**Investigations**

X-ray may show:

- No abnormality in the first 1 to 2 weeks
- Loss of bone density, or rarefaction, at about 2 weeks
- A thin white line on the surface of the infected part of the bone, suggesting periosteal reaction
- Later, a piece of dead bone, or sequestrum

Other investigations:

- Complete blood count
- ESR
- Blood culture and sensitivity
- ZN stain, GeneXpert, and culture if the lesion is suspicious
- Calcofluor stain for fungus

**Management**

Patients with suspected osteomyelitis need to be referred to hospital for appropriate management.

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Immobilise and splint the limb. | HC3 |
| Provide pain and fever relief with paracetamol or ibuprofen. | HC3 |
| Refer urgently to hospital. | HC3 |
| Admit and elevate the affected limb. | H |
| Give cloxacillin 500 mg IV every 6 hours for 2 weeks. Continue orally for at least 4 weeks, and up to 3 months if required. | H |
| Child: cloxacillin 50 mg/kg every 6 hours. | H |
| See pyogenic arthritis for other antibiotic treatments. | H |
| For osteomyelitis in sickle cell disease, see section 11.1.3. | H |
| Surgical intervention may be indicated. | RR |

Surgical intervention may include:

- Drainage of subperiosteal and soft tissue abscesses
- Drainage of intramedullary purulence
- Debridement of contiguous foci of infection, with antimicrobial therapy
- Excision of sequestra, meaning devitalised bone
- Surgery where there is failure to improve after 48 to 72 hours of antimicrobial therapy
- Surgery and antibiotics for chronic osteomyelitis


### 10.1.3 Pyomyositis

**ICD-10 CODE:** M60.0

Pyomyositis is a bacterial infection of muscle, commonly caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*.

**Causes**

- Bacterial infection, commonly *Staphylococcus aureus*

**Clinical features**

- Usually localised in one muscle
- Commonly affects large striated muscles
- Fever
- Painful swelling of the involved muscle
- Affected area is hot, swollen, and tender
- Fluctuation when pus forms

**Differential diagnosis**

- Cellulitis
- Boil
- Osteomyelitis
- Peritonitis, especially in pyomyositis of abdominal muscles

**Investigations**

- Full blood count
- Pus for culture and sensitivity
- Consider HIV infection

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| General management | Elevate and immobilise the affected limb. | HC3 |
| Antibiotic treatment | Give cloxacillin 500 mg IV or orally every 6 hours for 5 to 10 days. | HC3 / HC4 |
| Antibiotic treatment in children | Give cloxacillin 12.5 to 25 mg/kg per dose. | HC3 / HC4 |
| Early stage, when the muscle is indurated, hot, and swollen | Antibiotic treatment may be sufficient to resolve the infection. | HC3 / HC4 |
| When abscess has formed | Surgical drainage is the only effective treatment. | HC4 |
| After surgical drainage | Leave the wound open, pack, and clean daily. | HC4 |

### 10.1.4 Tuberculosis of the Spine (Pott's Disease)

**ICD-10 CODE:** A18.01

Tuberculous spondylitis, also called Pott's disease, is the most common form of skeletal TB. It usually affects the lower thoracic and upper lumbar region.

Infection begins with inflammation of the intervertebral joints and can spread to involve the adjacent vertebral body. Once two adjacent vertebrae are involved, infection can involve the adjoining intervertebral disc space, leading to vertebral collapse. Subsequent kyphosis can lead to cord compression and paraplegia.

**Causes**

- Chronic infection caused by mycobacteria

**Clinical features**

- Most common in young adults
- Local pain that increases in severity over weeks to months
- Pain may be associated with muscle spasm and rigidity
- Constitutional symptoms, such as fever and weight loss, are present in less than 40% of cases
- With progression and spread of disease, anterior collapse of affected vertebrae may lead to visible deformity, such as angular kyphosis or gibbus
- Risk of cord compression
- Weakness of the legs, also called Pott's paraplegia
- Visceral dysfunction

**Differential diagnosis**

- Staphylococcal spondylitis
- Brucellosis
- Metastatic lesion

**Investigations**

- Adequate history
- Careful clinical examination
- Spine X-ray, which may show:
    - Disc space narrowing
    - Paravertebral shadow
    - Single or multiple vertebral involvement
    - Destructive lesions of two or more vertebrae without new bone formation
    - Destruction of vertebral end-plates
- ESR, usually raised
- White blood cell count, usually within normal limits

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Rest the spine. | HC4 |
| Fit a spinal corset or plaster jacket for pain relief. | HC4 |
| Give TB treatment as per guidelines. See section 5.3 for more details. | HC4 |
| Surgical intervention may be warranted in selected patients. | RR |

Surgical intervention is warranted in patients with:

- Spinal disease and advanced neurological deficits
- Spinal disease and worsening neurological deficits progressing while on appropriate therapy
- Spinal disease and kyphosis greater than 40 degrees at presentation
- Chest wall cold abscess

## 10.2 Inflammatory / Degenerative Disorders

### 10.2.1 Rheumatoid Arthritis

**ICD-10 CODE:** M05

Rheumatoid arthritis is the most common form of chronic inflammatory joint disease. It mainly affects women. Attacks tend to be bilateral with symmetrical involvement and may cause joint destruction.

**Causes**

- Unknown origin
- Probably autoimmune

**Clinical features**

- Stiffness and pain in the joints
- Usually more than 3 joints are affected
- Joint involvement is usually symmetrical
- Symptoms are worse in the morning
- Joints are swollen, warm, inflamed, and sensitive to touch
- Fingers are most affected, especially the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints
- All small and medium-sized joints can be affected
- Hips and spine are rarely affected
- Extra-articular manifestations may include:
    - Mild fever
    - Weakness
    - Lethargy
    - Anorexia
    - Weight loss
    - Rheumatoid nodules, commonly on extensor surfaces such as the forearm below the joint
- The disease is chronic, with flare-ups, remission, and exacerbations
- In advanced cases, joint deformities may occur

**Differential diagnosis**

- Osteoarthritis
- Gout arthritis, especially in males
- Reactive arthritis

**Investigations**

- Full blood count
- ESR
- Rheumatoid factor
- Antinuclear factor
- X-ray of affected joints

**Management**

Goals of treatment are:

- Relief of symptoms
- Preservation of joint function
- Suppression of active disease
- Slowing progression of disease
- Prevention of structural damage and deformity
- Maintenance of the patient’s normal lifestyle

Symptomatic treatment can be started at lower level, but appropriate management requires referral for specialist care.

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Pain and inflammation in acute flare | Rest the affected joints. | HC2 |
| Pain and inflammation in acute flare | Give an NSAID such as ibuprofen 400 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Pain and inflammation in acute flare | Or give diclofenac 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Pain and inflammation in acute flare | Or give indomethacin 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| NSAID use | Long-term treatment is not advised because of toxicity and because NSAIDs do not modify disease progression. | HC2 |
| NSAID use | Consider gastroprotection with omeprazole 20 mg once daily. | HC3 |
| Severe acute inflammation | Give prednisolone 5 to 10 mg once daily in the morning. | RR |
| Corticosteroids | Corticosteroids slow disease progression but should not be used for long periods because of side effects. | RR |
| Corticosteroids | Use for treating acute symptoms and while waiting to start specific medicines. | RR |
| Disease-modifying treatment | Refer to a specialist for disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs. | RR |
| Disease-modifying treatment | Specialist options include methotrexate and chloroquine. | RR |
| Supportive care | Provide counselling and health education. | HC2 / HC3 |
| Supportive care | Advise weight loss and appropriate exercise or physiotherapy. | HC2 / HC3 |

### 10.2.2 Gout Arthritis

**ICD-10 CODE:** M10

Gout arthritis is an inflammatory arthritis caused by altered urate metabolism with deposition of urate salts in joints and, in advanced cases, other tissues.

**Causes**

- Altered urate metabolism
- Deposition of urate salts in joints
- Deposition of urate salts in other tissues in advanced cases

**Clinical features**

**Acute gout**

- Affected joint is hot, red, and swollen
- Mostly affects the big toe at the metatarsophalangeal joint, called podagra
- May occasionally start in other joints
- Sudden severe pain, often at night

**Chronic gout**

- Repeated acute attacks
- Progressive cartilage and bone erosion
- Deposition of tophi in soft tissue, such as:
    - Ear cartilage
    - Bursae
    - Tendon sheaths

**Differential diagnosis**

- Joint infection
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Injury
- Pseudogout
- Osteoarthritis

**Investigations**

- Joint aspiration for uric acid crystals viewed by polarising microscope
- X-ray of the affected joint or joints
- Serum uric acid, usually elevated

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Acute attack | Rest and immobilise the affected joint. | HC2 |
| Acute attack | Start an NSAID such as ibuprofen 400 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute attack | Or give indomethacin 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute attack | Or give diclofenac 50 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute attack | Continue NSAID treatment for the duration of the attack. | HC2 |
| If NSAIDs are contraindicated | Give prednisolone 40 mg once daily for 5 days. | HC4 |
| If NSAIDs are contraindicated | Or give colchicine 0.5 to 1 mg initially, followed by 0.5 mg every 2 to 3 hours until pain relief occurs or vomiting and diarrhoea occur. Maximum dose is 6 mg. Do not repeat the course within 3 days. | HC3 / H |
| Chronic gout | Advise weight reduction. | H |
| Chronic gout | Advise dietary control, including a healthy diet and limited alcohol consumption. Coffee may be beneficial. | H |
| Chronic gout | Avoid medicines that may increase uric acid, such as thiazide diuretics. | H |
| More than 2 attacks per year and/or complications such as renal stones or chronic tophaceous gout | Give allopurinol, starting at 100 mg. Increase monthly by 100 mg. Average maintenance dose is 300 mg. Maximum dose is 900 mg. Titrate to keep uric acid below 0.35 mmol/L. | H |
| Allopurinol use | Do not start allopurinol during an acute attack, but continue it if already started. | H |
| Prevention of acute attacks after starting allopurinol | Give prophylactic colchicine 0.5 mg every 12 hours for the first 3 months. | H |

> **Note**
> Do not use allopurinol to treat asymptomatic hyperuricaemia.
>
### 10.2.3 Osteoarthritis

**ICD-10 CODE:** M15–M19

Osteoarthritis is a degenerative joint disease with damage to articular cartilage. It is the commonest form of joint disease. The pathological changes in osteoarthritis are irreversible.

**Causes and risk factors**

- Previous injury
- Overweight
- Age

**Clinical features**

- May involve any joint
- Most commonly affects the hip, spine, and knees
- Usually not symmetrical
- Restriction of movement
- Pain on moving the joint
- Pain tends to be absent at rest
- Limp if lower limbs are affected
- Deformity
- Moderate tenderness
- Improvement with rest
- Deterioration with physical activity
- Deterioration with cold and wet weather conditions
- Joints are usually not swollen or warm
- There may be some accumulation of clear articular fluid

**Differential diagnosis**

- Gout
- Gouty arthritis
- Rheumatoid arthritis

**Investigations**

- Full blood count, usually normal
- ESR, usually normal
- X-ray of affected joint or joints

**Management**

Goals of treatment are:

- Pain relief
- Optimisation of function
- Minimisation of progression

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| General measures | Advise weight reduction. | HC2 |
| General measures | Encourage activity and regular exercise. | HC2 |
| General measures | Advise use of appropriate footwear and walking aids. | HC2 |
| Pain relief | Give paracetamol 1 g every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute exacerbation or severe pain | Give an NSAID, such as ibuprofen or diclofenac. Limit use to brief periods. | HC4 |
| Local pain relief | Give diclofenac 1% gel if available. | HC4 |
| Specialist treatment | Intra-articular steroids such as triamcinolone may be used by a specialist only, maximum 4 times per year. | RR |

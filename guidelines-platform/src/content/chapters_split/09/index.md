# Chapter 9: Mental, Neurological and Substance Use Disorders

## 9.1 Neurological Disorders

### 9.1.1 Epilepsy

**ICD-10 CODE:** G40

Epilepsy is a chronic condition characterised by recurrent unprovoked seizures. Seizures are caused by abnormal discharges in the brain and may present as convulsive or non-convulsive forms.

Convulsive epilepsy may present with sudden muscle contraction, causing the person to fall and become rigid, followed by alternating relaxation and rigidity of muscles, with or without loss of bowel or bladder control.

Non-convulsive epilepsy may present with changes in awareness, behaviour, emotions, or senses, such as taste, smell, vision, or hearing. These features may resemble mental health conditions and may therefore be confused with them.

Consider a diagnosis of epilepsy if a person has had at least two seizures in the last calendar year on two different days.

Seizures during an acute event, such as meningitis or acute traumatic brain injury, are not epilepsy.

**Causes**

- Genetic causes
- Congenital malformation
- Birth asphyxia
- Brain tumour
- Brain infections
- Cysticercosis
- Trauma, either acute or in the past
- Metabolic disorders
- In some cases, no specific cause can be identified

**Clinical features**

Clinical features depend on the type of epilepsy.

| Type of epilepsy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Generalised epilepsy** | Seizure involves the whole brain. Consciousness is lost at onset. |
| **Tonic-clonic, grand mal, or convulsive epilepsy** | May commence with a warning sensation, such as sound, light, or abdominal pain, called an aura.<br><br>There may be a sharp cry followed by loss of consciousness and falling.<br><br>Tonic contraction, or rigidity, of muscles occurs, followed by jerking movements, called the clonic phase.<br><br>There may be incontinence of urine or faeces, frothing, and tongue biting.<br><br>A period of deep sleep follows. |
| **Absence seizures, or petit mal** | Mainly a disorder of children.<br><br>The attack is characterised by a brief loss of consciousness, usually 5 to 10 seconds, during which posture is retained but other activities cease.<br><br>The child has a vacant stare.<br><br>Previous activities are resumed at the end of the attack.<br><br>Several attacks may occur in a single day. |
| **Atonic or tonic seizures, or drop attacks** | Sudden loss of muscular tone of brief duration, usually about 15 seconds, with consciousness maintained.<br><br>Alternatively, there may be sudden stiffening of muscles. |
| **Myoclonic epilepsy** | Abnormal jerking movements, usually occurring in the limbs, but may involve the whole body. |
| **Focal epilepsy** | Seizure activity starts in one area of the brain. |
| **Simple focal seizure** | The patient remains alert but has abnormal sensory, motor, psychic, or autonomic manifestations.<br><br>Examples include jerking of a limb, déjà vu, nausea, strange taste or smell, sweating, flushing, gastric sensation, motor contraction, or sensory change in a particular part of the body. |
| **Complex focal seizure** | Altered awareness and behaviour, such as confusion or repetitive movements. |
| **Status epilepticus** | A convulsive state in which convulsions last more than 30 minutes, or several epileptic convulsions occur in succession without recovery of consciousness in between, or convulsions are not responsive to two doses of diazepam. It is a medical emergency. |

**Differential diagnosis**

- Syncope, or fainting
- Hypoglycaemia
- Hypocalcaemia
- Conversion disorder, previously known as hysteria
- Hyperventilation
- Panic attacks

**Investigations**

- Complete medical and mental health assessment
- Electroencephalogram (EEG)
    - Useful in petit mal and focal seizures
    - To be done at specialist level, regional referral or national referral level
- Other investigations guided by suspected cause

**General principles**

- All suspected cases of non-convulsive epilepsy should be confirmed and treated by a specialist.
- Convulsive epilepsy can be diagnosed at hospital or HC3 level, but medicine refills should be available at lower levels.
- One brief isolated seizure does not need further treatment, but review after 3 months and reassess.
- Treat patients with repeated episodes according to the case definition.
- Treatment can effectively control epilepsy in most cases.
- Treatment should include psychological and social support.
- Start with a single anti-epileptic medicine.
- Start with low doses and increase gradually according to response.
- If a patient has been seizure-free for 2 years, consider gradual stopping of medication.

Commonly used antiepileptic medicines include:

- Generalised tonic-clonic seizures:
    - Children below 2 years: phenobarbital or carbamazepine
    - Children above 2 years: carbamazepine or valproate
- Absence seizures:
    - Valproate or ethosuximide

> **Caution**
> Avoid phenobarbital and phenytoin in children with intellectual disability and/or behavioural problems.
>
**Management**

| Situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| First aid for acute seizure | Do not restrain the patient. | HC2 |
| First aid for acute seizure | Do not put anything in the mouth. | HC2 |
| First aid for acute seizure | Protect the person from injury by ensuring they are in a safe place, away from fire or other hazards. | HC2 |
| First aid for acute seizure | Do not leave the patient alone. Seek help if possible. | HC2 |
| After the seizure | Check airway, breathing, and circulation. While the patient is unconscious, put the person in the recovery position, on their side. | HC2 |
| Most seizures | Most seizures resolve spontaneously. | HC3 |

**Status epilepticus**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Give dextrose 50%, 1 mL/kg in adults. | HC4 |
| In children, give dextrose 10%, 5 mL/kg. | HC4 |
| Give diazepam 10 mg IV or rectally. | HC4 |
| Child: diazepam 0.05 mg/kg rectally or 0.02 mg/kg IV. Repeat dose after 5 to 10 minutes if seizures persist. | HC4 |
| If not responsive, consider phenobarbital 10 to 15 mg/kg slowly IV. Dilute the solution with 10 times its volume of water for injection and give very slowly, at a rate of 0.1 mg/minute. | HC4 |
| Monitor blood pressure and respiration. Be ready to administer IV fluids if hypotension develops and ventilate with Ambu bag if respiratory depression occurs. | HC4 |
| Alternatively, give phenytoin 15 to 18 mg/kg over 1 hour. | HC4 |
| Phenytoin can cause severe tissue damage, so use a good IV line. | HC4 |
| If still not responsive, give another medicine if available, or add phenytoin 10 mg/kg over 30 minutes. | HC4 |
| Monitor for respiratory depression. | HC4 |

**Long-term management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Start with a single anti-epileptic medicine. The effective dose must be reached progressively and the patient monitored for tolerance and side effects. Aim for the lowest dose able to control, or prevent, seizures. | HC3 |
| If treatment is ineffective, meaning less than 50% reduction in seizures, try another monotherapy. Slowly reduce the current antiepileptic medicine and introduce the new one. | HC3 |
| If high doses with side effects are required and seizures are infrequent, less than complete control may be the goal. | HC3 |
| Follow up monthly until stable, then every 3 months. | HC3 |
| Warn the patient that treatment interruptions can trigger seizures or even status epilepticus. | HC3 |
| If there has been no seizure for 2 years and there is no known cause such as head trauma or infection, consider stopping treatment gradually over 2 months. Discuss this with the patient. | HC3 |
| If two monotherapy trials fail, refer to a specialist. | HC3 |

**Common anti-epileptic medicines**

| Medicine | Use and dosing | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbamazepine** | Effective in generalised tonic-clonic seizures and focal seizures.<br><br>Given twice daily. Steady state is reached in 8 days.<br><br>Adult: start with 100 to 200 mg daily and increase in 100 mg increments every 1 to 2 weeks to a maintenance dose of 400 to 1400 mg daily.<br><br>Child: start with 5 mg/kg/day and use a maintenance dose of 10 to 20 mg/kg/day in divided doses.<br><br>Side effects include skin rash, diplopia, blurred vision, ataxia, nausea. | HC4 |
| **Phenobarbital** | Effective for tonic-clonic seizures and focal seizures, but it is sedative in adults and may cause behavioural disturbances and hyperkinesia in children. It may be tried for atypical absences, atonic seizures, and tonic seizures.<br><br>Given once daily in the evening to reduce drowsiness.<br><br>Adult: start with 1 mg/kg, usually 60 mg daily, for 2 weeks. If not controlled, increase to 2 mg/kg, usually 120 mg, for 2 months. If still not controlled, increase to 3 mg/kg, usually 180 mg.<br><br>Child: start with 2 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks. If not controlled, increase to 3 mg/kg for 2 months. If still not controlled, increase up to a maximum of 6 mg/kg/day.<br><br>It takes 2 to 3 weeks for the medicine to achieve steady blood levels, so assess effect only after this period.<br><br>Side effects include drowsiness, lethargy, hyperactivity and irritability in children, skin rash, confusion in elderly patients, and depression. | HC3 |
| **Phenytoin** | Effective in all forms of epilepsy except absences.<br><br>Adult: start with 150 to 200 mg daily as a single dose or in 2 divided doses. Maintenance dose is 200 to 400 mg daily.<br><br>Child: start with 3 to 4 mg/kg. Maintenance dose is 3 to 8 mg/kg/day, maximum 300 mg daily.<br><br>Increase slowly by 25 to 30 mg every 2 weeks.<br><br>Side effects include drowsiness, ataxia, slurred speech, blurred vision, twitching, confusion, gum hyperplasia, blood abnormalities, rash, and hepatitis. | HC4 |
| **Ethosuximide** | Effective in absence seizures.<br><br>Child above 6 years: initially 500 mg daily in 2 divided doses. Increase if necessary by 250 mg every 5 to 7 days, up to a usual daily dose of 1 to 1.5 g in 2 divided doses.<br><br>Child 1 month to 6 years: initially 250 mg as a single dose at night. Increase gradually every 5 to 7 days as required to the usual dose of 20 to 40 mg/kg daily in 2 divided doses.<br><br>Side effects include gastrointestinal disorders, blood disorders, gum hyperplasia, and drowsiness. | RR |

> **Note**
> In children, look for associated intellectual disability or behavioural problems.
>
> If intellectual disability or behavioural problems are present, consider carbamazepine or valproate. Avoid phenobarbital and phenytoin, and manage the associated intellectual disability or behavioural problem.
>
> All pregnant women with epilepsy should be referred to a specialist for appropriate management.
>
**Health education**

- Provide health and psycho-education to patients, carers, and the community.
- Advise on management of seizures and safety precautions.
- In children, look for and manage associated intellectual disability or behavioural problems.

**Prevention**

- Promote good antenatal care and delivery.
- Avoid causative factors.

### 9.1.2 Nodding Disease

**ICD-10 CODE:** G40.4

Nodding disease is an unexplained neurological condition characterised by episodes of repetitive dropping forward of the head, often accompanied by other seizure-like activity, such as convulsions or staring spells.

The condition predominantly affects children aged 5 to 15 years and has been reported in South Sudan, especially the states of Western and Central Equatoria, northern Uganda, and southern Tanzania.

**Cause**

The cause is not yet certain, but a consistent association with onchocerciasis has been found.

Other associated factors include:

- Malnutrition
- Pyridoxine deficiency

**Clinical features**

- Starts at age 5 to 7 years in a previously normal child
- Early symptoms include problems in concentration and thinking
- “Nodding” starts, which is a type of seizure, also called atonic seizure, often triggered by eating or cold temperatures
- Cognitive impairment appears
- Neurological deterioration, delayed puberty, and growth retardation progress until the child becomes mentally and physically disabled

**Investigations**

- No diagnostic investigations have been identified

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic epilepsy | Supportive care. | HC4 |
| Chronic epilepsy | Give antiepileptic medicines as above, including valproate and phenobarbital. | HC4 |

### 9.1.3 Headache

**ICD-10 CODE:** R51

Headache is a common complaint and cause of disability. Pain can be of varying intensity and may affect different areas of the head.

**Causes**

- Facial and frontal headache: sinusitis, eye problems, oropharyngeal disorders
- Temporal headache: severe hypertension, stress, ear disorders, subarachnoid haemorrhage
- Top of the head: stress, tension
- Unilateral, or one-sided headache: migraine
- Whole head: malaria, meningitis, severe hypertension, dehydration
- Back of the head, occiput and neck: meningitis, malaria, refractive eye problems, neck trauma or sprain, tension

**Danger signs**

| Sign or symptom | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Recent trauma to the head | Intracranial bleeding |
| Head injury | Intracranial bleeding |
| High fever | Malaria, meningitis, or other infections |
| Acute onset, severe headache | Intracranial bleeding |
| Chronic worsening headache | Tumours, hypertension |
| Altered consciousness and/or focal neurological symptoms and/or seizure | Tumour, intracranial bleeding, intracranial infection |

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Investigate and treat the cause if found or possible. | HC2 |
| If there are any danger signs, refer to hospital for further assessment. | HC2 |
| Follow the pain ladder for control of symptoms. | HC2 |


#### 9.1.3.1 Migraine

**ICD-10 CODE:** G43

Migraine is a periodic severe headache, usually unilateral, which may occur with or without an aura, meaning neurological warning signs. It is commonly associated with nausea and/or vomiting.

**Causes**

The cause is unknown, but migraine is thought to be linked to:

- Familial factors
- Craniovascular disorders

Migraine may be precipitated by:

- Stress
- Anxiety
- Menstruation
- Flashing lights
- Hunger
- Lack of sleep
- Oestrogens, including combined oral contraceptives
- Perfumes
- Tyramine-containing foods, such as red wine, cheese, and chocolate

**Clinical features**

- Warning signs, or aura, such as visual or sensory symptoms, including flashing lights, preceding the start of the headache
- Migraine with warning signs is called migraine with aura, although warning signs are not always present
- Moderate to severe episodic unilateral headache
- Throbbing or pulsating headache
- Nausea and vomiting
- Sensitivity to light and sound

**Differential diagnosis**

- Any cause of headache
- Conversion disorder, previously known as hysteria

**Investigations**

No specific investigations are needed unless another cause is suspected.

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Acute episode | Give paracetamol 1 g every 6 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute episode | Or give ibuprofen 400 mg every 6 to 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Acute episode | Or give acetylsalicylic acid 300 to 900 mg every 4 to 6 hours, maximum 4 g daily. | HC2 |
| Severe migraine, or migraine not responding to the above treatment | Give diclofenac 75 mg IM. | HC4 |
| Severe migraine, or migraine not responding to the above treatment | Plus metoclopramide 10 mg IM or IV for nausea and vomiting. | HC4 |
| Severe migraine, or migraine not responding to the above treatment | Or give ergotamine 2 mg sublingually, then 1 to 2 mg hourly to a maximum of 6 mg in 24 hours. | RR |
| Severe migraine, or migraine not responding to the above treatment | Or give sumatriptan 50 mg, repeat after 2 hours if necessary, maximum 300 mg in 24 hours. | RR |
| Prophylaxis, if there are more than 3 attacks per month and/or functional impairment | Give amitriptyline 10 to 75 mg at night. | HC3 |
| Prophylaxis, if there are more than 3 attacks per month and/or functional impairment | Or give propranolol 40 to 80 mg every 12 hours. | HC4 |

**Prevention**

- Avoid precipitating factors.

### 9.1.4 Dementia

**ICD-10 CODE:** F01, F03

Dementia is a chronic, slowly progressive organic mental disorder characterised by progressive loss of memory and cognitive function, with difficulty carrying out everyday activities.

**Causes**

- Primary degeneration of the brain
- Vascular disorders
- Infections, such as syphilis, TB, HIV/AIDS, and meningitis
- Metabolic disorders, such as hypothyroidism
- Vitamin B12 deficiency
- Vitamin B1 deficiency
- Brain trauma, including chronic subdural haematoma and hydrocephalus
- Toxic agents, such as carbon monoxide and alcohol

**Clinical features**

- Impairment of short-term and long-term memory
- Impaired judgement
- Poor abstract thinking
- Language disturbances, such as aphasia
- Personality changes
- Apathy or withdrawal
- Anxiety or depression associated with failing memory
- Aggressive behaviour
- Wandering in later stages
- Incontinence in later stages

**Differential diagnosis**

- Normal ageing
- Delirium
- Chronic psychosis
- Depression

**Investigations**

Investigations should be guided by history and clinical picture to establish the cause.

Recommended assessment may include:

- Thorough physical examination
- Neurological examination
- Mental state examination
- Thyroid hormone tests
- RPR
- Vitamin B12 levels
- Other tests as indicated

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Where possible, identify and treat the cause. | HC2 |
| Provide psychosocial interventions. | HC2 |
| Provide psychoeducation to family members about the illness and the importance of following a regular routine programme. | HC2 |
| Provide regular orientation information. | HC2 |
| Create an environment that supports activities of daily living. | HC2 |
| Assess for and treat other co-occurring health problems, such as depression and HIV. | HC2 |
| Give donepezil 5 mg once daily initially. Increase to 10 mg after 4 to 6 weeks if appropriate. Preferably give at bedtime. | H |
| Alternatively, give memantine 10 mg once daily initially. Increase to 20 mg after 4 to 6 weeks if appropriate. Preferably give at bedtime. | H |
| If the patient is restless and agitated, give haloperidol 0.5 to 1 mg every 8 hours, with a higher dose at night if required. | HC4 |
| Alternatively, give risperidone 0.5 to 1 mg once daily, preferably at night. | HC4 |
| Adjust antipsychotic dose according to response and review regularly. Monitor for extrapyramidal side effects and treat with benzhexol 2 mg every 12 hours if necessary. | HC4 |

> **Note**
> Donepezil and memantine only slow progression of symptoms. They do not cure dementia.
>
> **Caution**
> Avoid diazepam. It can lead to falls and is often not effective.
>
**Prevention**

- Avoid and treat preventable causes.

### 9.1.5 Parkinsonism

**ICD-10 CODE:** G20, G21

Parkinsonism is a syndrome characterised by tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, meaning slow movement, and postural disturbances. It is due to primary degeneration or damage to particular areas of the brain, especially the basal ganglia.

**Causes**

Primary parkinsonism:

- Cause is unknown

Secondary parkinsonism may be caused by:

- Infections, such as sleeping sickness and syphilis
- Poisoning, such as manganese and carbon monoxide
- Medicines, such as chlorpromazine and haloperidol
- Vascular disorders
- Intracranial tumour
- Trauma

**Clinical features**

- Non-intentional tremor
- Muscle rigidity
- Slowness of voluntary movement
- Walking with short, quick steps, also called shuffling gait
- Vacant facial expression, also called mask face
- Excessive salivation
- Urinary incontinence, which may sometimes occur
- Variable cognitive impairment

**Differential diagnosis**

- Essential tremor, meaning isolated intentional tremor, usually benign
- Thyrotoxicosis
- Dementia
- Depression

**Investigations**

- Good history and clinical examination

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Give levodopa-carbidopa 10/100 mg. The dose can be increased to 25/100 mg. | RR |
| Start with 1 tablet every 8 hours. Specialist-only management. | RR |
| For drug-induced parkinsonism, give benzhexol 2 to 15 mg daily in 1 to 3 divided doses. | HC2 |
| Initially give benzhexol 1 mg daily, then increase by 2 mg increments at intervals of 3 to 5 days. | HC2 |
| Usual benzhexol dose is 6 to 10 mg daily in 3 to 4 divided doses. Doses of 12 to 15 mg daily may be required. | HC2 |

> **Caution**
> Benzhexol side effects include dry mouth, constipation, palpitations, urine retention, confusion, and agitation, especially in elderly patients.
>
> Do not give benzhexol routinely to patients on antipsychotic medicines in the absence of Parkinson-like symptoms.
>
### 9.1.6 Delirium

**ICD-10 CODE:** F05

Delirium, also called acute confusional state, is a clinical syndrome usually with acute onset. It involves abnormalities in thought and perception and a fluctuating level of consciousness. It is caused by impaired brain function resulting from diffuse physiological change.

**Causes**

- Infections, such as malaria, trypanosomiasis, syphilis, meningitis, rabies, typhoid fever, and HIV/AIDS
- Pneumonia and urinary tract infections in elderly patients
- Intoxication with, or withdrawal from, alcohol or other substances of dependence
- Some medicines, such as anticonvulsants and neuropsychiatric medicines
- Cerebral pathology, such as head trauma and tumour
- Severe anaemia
- Dehydration
- Electrolyte imbalances
- Hyperglycaemia

**Clinical features**

- Acute onset of mental confusion with associated disorientation, developing within hours or a few days
- Attention, concentration, and memory for recent events are impaired
- Reduced ability to think coherently
- Reasoning and problem-solving are difficult or impossible
- Illusions and hallucinations are common
- Symptoms tend to fluctuate
- Patients may feel better during the day and worse at night
- Some patients may present with reduced activity and/or movement, also called hypoactive delirium

**Differential diagnosis**

- Acute psychosis

**Investigations**

Investigations should be guided by history and physical examination, and should aim at identifying the cause.

> **Note**
> Drug history is very important.
>
Suggested investigations include:

- Complete blood count
- Blood glucose
- Rapid diagnostic test, where malaria is suspected
- Renal function tests
- Electrolytes

**Management**

Due to the complexity of underlying conditions, patients with acute confusional state should be referred to hospital for appropriate management and investigation.

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Identify and treat the cause, such as substance and alcohol use disorders, diabetes, head injury, or infections such as malaria, UTI, and pneumonia in older people. | H |
| Provide supportive treatment. | H |
| Ensure hydration. | H |
| Control fever. | H |
| Provide a safe and quiet environment. | H |
| Apply constant monitoring. | H |
| Withdraw unnecessary medicines. | H |
| Keep medicines such as sedatives and antipsychotics to the minimum necessary. | H |
| If the patient is agitated and acutely disturbed, give haloperidol 5 mg IM. Repeat after 60 minutes if necessary. | H |
| Continue with haloperidol 1.25 to 5 mg every 8 to 12 hours if needed. | H |
| Or give chlorpromazine 25 to 50 mg every 8 to 12 hours, IM or oral. | H |
| Or give trifluoperazine 5 to 10 mg every 12 hours. | H |

**Prevention**

- Early diagnosis and treatment of the underlying cause


## 9.2 Psychiatric and Substance Use Disorders

### 9.2.1 Anxiety

**ICD-10 CODE:** F40–F48

Anxiety is a normal physiological response which enables a person to take steps to deal with a threat. When anxiety is prolonged or interferes with normal functioning, it constitutes the clinical condition of an anxiety disorder.

**Causes**

- Not fully understood
- External traumatic events may trigger anxiety in predisposed people
- Association with other mental conditions, such as depression
- Alcohol and substance abuse

**Types and clinical features**

Generalised anxiety:

- Unrealistic and excessive worry about almost everything

Panic attacks:

- Episodes of sudden onset of intense apprehension or fear
- Anxiety symptoms usually peak within 10 to 15 minutes
- Symptoms resolve within a few minutes to one hour

Phobia:

- Excessive fear of a known stimulus, object, or situation
- Examples include animals, water, and confined spaces
- The person consciously avoids the object or situation

Each of the above clinical types may have one or more of the following features:

- Sleep problems
- Mood problems
- Concentration problems
- Palpitations
- Dizziness
- Shortness of breath
- Shakiness or tremors
- Excessive sweating
- Easily frightened
- Urinary frequency, hesitancy, or urgency
- Diarrhoea

**Differential diagnosis**

- Hyperthyroidism
- Hypoglycaemia
- Phaeochromocytoma

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Provide psychosocial interventions, including counselling, individual psychotherapy, or group psychotherapy. | HC2 |
| For an acute episode or intense prolonged anxiety, give benzodiazepines, such as diazepam 5 mg once or twice daily. | HC4 |
| If necessary, increase diazepam to 15 to 30 mg daily in divided doses. | HC4 |
| In elderly patients, give alprazolam 0.25 to 0.5 mg twice daily initially. If necessary, increase to 3 to 6 mg daily in divided doses. | HC4 |
| If alprazolam is not available, give half the usual adult diazepam dose. | HC4 |
| Duration of benzodiazepine therapy should be 1 to 2 weeks, tapering off to zero within 6 weeks. | HC4 |
| If response is poor, refer to a specialist. | HC4 |
| For long-term management of anxiety disorder, give fluoxetine 20 mg once daily. | HC4 |
| Or give sertraline 50 mg once daily. | HC4 |
| Continue antidepressant treatment for 4 to 6 weeks, then evaluate response. | HC4 |

> **Caution**
> Diazepam is addictive. Abrupt cessation can cause withdrawal symptoms. Use it for short periods and gradually reduce the dose. Avoid alcohol.
>
> **Note**
> Diazepam is not appropriate for treating depression, phobic or obsessional states, or chronic psychoses.
>
> Antidepressants may be useful in managing panic disorders and other anxiety disorders that require long-term treatment.
>
**Prevention**

- Good personality development
- Good stress management

#### 9.2.2.2 Suicidal Behaviour / Self Harm

**ICD-10 CODES:** T14.91, Z91.5

Suicidal behaviour is an emergency and requires immediate attention. It refers to a conscious act of self-destruction that the individual views as the best solution to their distress. It is usually associated with feelings of hopelessness, helplessness, and conflict between survival and death.

Self-harm is a broader term referring to intentional self-injury or poisoning, which may or may not involve intent to die.

**Causes and risk factors**

Physical illness may increase risk, including:

- HIV/AIDS
- Head injury
- Malignancy
- Body disfigurement
- Chronic pain

Psychiatric disorders may increase risk, including:

- Depression
- Chronic psychosis
- Dementia
- Alcohol and substance use disorders
- Personality disorders
- Epilepsy

Risk is high in the following situations:

- Age above 45 years
- Alcohol and substance use
- Previous suicide attempt
- Family history of suicide
- Recent loss or disappointment
- Current mental illness, such as depression or psychosis
- Evidence of violent behaviour
- Previous psychiatric admission

Risk may be lower where the patient is:

- Below 45 years of age
- Married or in a stable interpersonal relationship
- Employed
- In good physical health

**Clinical features**

Patients may present in one of the following situations:

- Current suicidal behaviour or act of self-harm
- Imminent risk of suicidal behaviour or self-harm
- No imminent risk, but recent thoughts, plans, or previous self-harm behaviour

A situation of imminent risk may include:

- Current thoughts or plans of suicide or self-harm
- History of thoughts or plans of suicide or self-harm in the last 1 month
- Acts of self-harm or suicide attempt in the last 1 year
- Agitation, violence, emotional distress, poor communication, social isolation, or hopelessness

A situation of no imminent risk may include:

- Thoughts or plans of suicide or self-harm in the last 1 month
- Acts of self-harm or suicide attempt in the last 1 year, but the person is not acutely distressed

**Investigations**

- Take a complete medical, social, and family history.
- Ask the patient about suicidal or self-harm thoughts, plans, acts, and reasons.
- Asking about self-harm or suicide does not increase the risk. It may help the patient feel understood and considered.
- First establish a good relationship with the patient before asking.
- Always assess risk of suicide and self-harm in patients with:
    - Depression
    - Mania
    - Psychosis
    - Alcohol and substance abuse
    - Dementia
    - Behavioural or developmental disorders
    - Chronic pain
    - Severe emotional distress

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Admit the patient and treat any medical complications. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Keep the patient in a secure and supportive environment. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Do not leave the patient alone. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Remove means of self-harm. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Provide continuous monitoring. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Offer or activate psychosocial support. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Consult a mental health specialist. | HC4 |
| Acute suicidal behaviour, act of self-harm, or imminent risk | Treat any medical or mental condition present. | HC4 |
| No imminent risk | Offer or activate psychosocial support. | HC4 |
| No imminent risk | Refer to a mental health specialist for further assessment. | HC4 |
| No imminent risk | Establish regular follow-up. | HC4 |

> **Note**
> Suicidal behaviour is less frequent in children and adolescents, but risk is increased where there is disturbed family background, such as death of parents or divorce, use of alcohol or other drugs, physical illness, or psychiatric disorder.
>
### 9.2.3 Bipolar Disorder (Mania)

**ICD-10 CODES:** F30, F31

Bipolar disorder is a disorder of mood control characterised by episodes in which a person's mood and activity level are significantly disturbed.

In some occasions, there is elevation of mood and increased energy and activity, called mania. In other occasions, there is lowering of mood and decreased energy and activity, called depression.

Characteristically, recovery is complete between episodes.

**Causes**

- Biological factors
- Genetic factors
- Environmental factors

**Clinical features**

The patient can present in:

- An acute manic episode
- A depressive episode
- Between episodes

**Mania**

Clinical features of mania may include:

- Elevated, expansive, or irritable mood
- Increased activity or subjective experience of increased energy
- Increased talkativeness
- Flight of ideas
- Increased self-image, self-esteem, or grandiosity
- Decreased need for sleep
- Distractibility
- Impulsive or reckless behaviour, extravagance, partying, and increased spending
- Increased sexual drive, sociability, and goal-directed behaviour
- Increased appetite, but weight loss may occur due to overactivity
- Auditory and visual hallucinations may be present

**Depression**

Features may be as described under depression, but with a history of manic episode.

A high index of suspicion for bipolar disorder is needed in early-onset depression with a family history of bipolar illness.

**Differential diagnosis**

- Organic mental state, such as drug or alcohol intoxication
- Delirium
- Chronic psychosis

**Investigations**

- Take a good medical, social, and personal history.
- Assess for acute state of mania.
- If depressive symptoms are present, investigate for previous manic episodes.
- Assess for other medical or mental conditions, including alcohol or substance abuse, dementia, suicide, and self-harm.

Patients with suspected bipolar disorder should be referred for specialist assessment.

**Management**

|---|---|---|

### 9.2.4 Psychosis

**ICD-10 CODE:** F20–F29

Psychosis is a mental condition characterised by distortions of thinking and perception, as well as inappropriate or narrowed range of emotions.

**Causes**

The cause is not fully known, but psychosis is associated with biological, genetic, and environmental factors.

**Clinical features**

Any one or more of the following may be diagnostic:

- Delusions, meaning abnormal, fixed, false beliefs
- Excessive and unwarranted suspicions, which may be multiple, fragmented, or bizarre
- Disconnected ideas with vague or incoherent speech
- Speech that is inadequate in content
- Hallucinations, such as hearing voices or seeing things that are not witnessed by others
- Severe behavioural abnormalities, such as agitation, disorganised behaviour, excitement, inactivity, or overactivity
- Disturbance of emotions, such as marked apathy
- Disconnection between reported emotions and observed affect
- Inappropriate mood
- Difficulty forming and sustaining relationships
- Social withdrawal
- Neglect of usual responsibilities

Chronic psychosis or schizophrenia is suggested by:

- Symptoms of psychosis lasting 3 or more months
- Deterioration in social, general, and occupational functioning

**Differential diagnosis**

- Alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
- Drug intoxication or withdrawal
- Organic delirium
- Dementia
- Mood disorders

**Investigations**

- Take a good social, personal, and family history.
- Investigate for infectious diseases, such as HIV, where clinically indicated.
- Other investigations should be guided by history and clinical findings.

**Management**

#### 9.2.4.1 Postnatal Psychosis

**ICD-10 CODE:** F53, Postpartum

Postnatal psychosis is a psychiatric emergency occurring after delivery.

**Risk factors**

- First child
- Previous episode of postnatal psychosis
- Previous major psychiatric history
- Family history of mental illness
- Inadequate psychosocial support during pregnancy
- Infections in the early puerperium

**Clinical features**

- Symptoms usually develop within the first 2 postpartum weeks
- Symptoms may develop as early as 48 to 72 hours after delivery
- The condition may resemble a rapidly evolving manic or mixed episode
- Restlessness
- Insomnia
- Irritability
- Rapidly shifting depressed or elated mood
- Disorganised behaviour
- Delusional beliefs relating to the infant
- Auditory hallucinations
- High risk of harm to the mother or infant

**Differential diagnosis**

- Depression with psychotic features
- Mania
- Chronic psychosis

**Investigations**

- Good history
- Physical assessment
- Psychiatric assessment

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Treat as a psychiatric emergency. Admit to hospital. | H |
| Treat any identifiable cause or precipitant, such as infection. | H |
| Give haloperidol 10 mg or chlorpromazine 200 mg by intramuscular injection or orally every 8 to 12 hours. | H |
| Monitor response to medication and adjust dosage accordingly. | H |
| If restless and agitated, add diazepam 5 to 10 mg by slow IV infusion or rectally. Repeat after 10 minutes if still agitated. | H |
| Continue diazepam tablets 5 mg every 12 hours until calm. | H |
| Refer to a specialist. | H |

> **Note**
> Postnatal psychoses are no different from other similar psychoses. Give concurrent psychosocial interventions and drug therapy.
>
**Prevention**

- Proper antenatal screening
- Good psychosocial support
- Early detection and treatment
- Adherence to treatment for current mental illness, such as depression, bipolar disorder, or chronic psychosis


### 9.2.5 Alcohol Use Disorders

**ICD-10 CODE:** F10

Alcohol use disorders are conditions resulting from different patterns of alcohol consumption, including acute alcohol intoxication, harmful alcohol use, alcohol dependence syndrome, and alcohol withdrawal state.

**Causes**

- No single cause is usually identified.
- A combination of factors usually leads to alcohol use disorders.

**Risk / predisposing factors**

- Genetic factors
- Social and environmental factors, including availability
- Stress and peer pressure
- Personality disorders

**Clinical features**

**Acute intoxication**

Acute intoxication is a transient condition following intake of alcohol, resulting in disturbances of:

- Consciousness
- Cognition
- Perception
- Affect
- Behaviour

**Harmful alcohol use**

Harmful alcohol use is a pattern of alcohol consumption that is causing damage to health, either physical, such as liver disease, or mental, such as depressive disorder.

It may also cause problems in the person's social, occupational, or other important areas of life.

Criteria include:

- More than five drinks on any given occasion in the last 12 months
- More than two drinks per day
- Drinking every day
- The patient consumes more alcohol than recommended but does not yet meet criteria for alcohol dependence

> **Caution**
> Alcohol consumption during pregnancy is extremely harmful to the baby. It can cause foetal harm.
>
**Alcohol dependence**

Alcohol dependence is a disorder characterised by the need to take large daily amounts of alcohol for adequate functioning. Alcohol use becomes a much higher priority for the individual than other behaviours that once had greater value.

Complications may include:

- Malnutrition
- Thiamine deficiency, causing Wernicke encephalopathy
- Liver disease
- Chronic pancreatitis
- Peptic ulcer
- Cardiomyopathy
- Neuropathy
- Head trauma

**Alcohol withdrawal**

Alcohol withdrawal symptoms occur after stopping alcohol following prolonged daily use. Symptoms may occur from 6 hours to 6 days after stopping alcohol.

Symptoms include:

- Tremor in hands
- Sweating
- Vomiting
- Tachycardia
- Hypertension
- Agitation
- Anxiety
- Headache
- Seizure
- Confusion in severe cases

**Diagnostic criteria for alcohol dependence**

If three or more of the following features are present, alcohol dependence is likely:

- A strong desire to take alcohol
- Difficulty controlling alcohol use in terms of onset, termination, or levels of use
- A physiological withdrawal state when alcohol use has ceased or reduced
- Evidence of tolerance, where increased doses of alcohol are required to achieve effects originally produced by lower doses
- Progressive neglect of alternative pleasures or interests because of alcohol use
- Alcohol use persists despite clear evidence of harmful consequences, such as liver damage, depression, cognitive impairment, loss of job, friends, or relationships

**Differential diagnosis**

- Abuse of other psychoactive substances
- Depression
- Chronic psychosis, often co-existing

**Investigations**

- Complete blood count
- Liver enzymes
- Social investigations

Blood tests may show:

- Elevated mean cell volume (MCV)
- Elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)

**Management**

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| Acute intoxication, withdrawal, and Wernicke encephalopathy | See section 1.3.12. |  |
| Harmful alcohol consumption | Provide counselling and advice. | HC3 |
| Harmful alcohol consumption | Investigate and treat concurrent medical or psychiatric illness, such as dementia, depression, anxiety, psychosis, or seizures. | HC3 |
| Harmful alcohol consumption | Follow up and refer if the patient does not improve. | HC3 |

**Prevention**

- Provide health education on the dangers of alcohol abuse.
- Reduce accessibility to alcohol.

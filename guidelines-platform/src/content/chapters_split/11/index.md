# Chapter 11: Blood Diseases and Blood Transfusion Guidelines

## 11.1 Blood Diseases

### 11.1.1 Anaemia

Anaemia is a reduction in haemoglobin concentration below the normal range for age, sex, and pregnancy status. It is not a final diagnosis. Careful history, physical examination, and laboratory tests are essential to determine the cause.

**Normal haemoglobin levels by age**

| Category | Normal value | Mild anaemia | Moderate anaemia | Severe anaemia |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Men >15 years | >13 g/dL | 11–12.9 g/dL | 8–10.9 g/dL | <8 g/dL |
| Women | >12 g/dL | 11–11.9 g/dL | 8–10.9 g/dL | <8 g/dL |
| Pregnant women | >11 g/dL | 10–10.9 g/dL | 7–9.9 g/dL | <7 g/dL |
| Child 12–14 years | >12 g/dL | 11–11.9 g/dL | 8–10.9 g/dL | <8 g/dL |
| Child 5–11 years | >11.5 g/dL | 11–11.5 g/dL | 8–10.9 g/dL | <8 g/dL |
| Child 6 months–5 years | >11 g/dL | 10–10.9 g/dL | 7–9.9 g/dL | <7 g/dL |

_Source: WHO/NMH/NHD/MNM/11.1._

**Reference range in newborns and infants**

| Age | Normal range |
|---|---:|
| Birth | >13.5 g/dL |
| 2 weeks | >12.5 g/dL |
| 1–6 months | >9.5 g/dL |

_Adapted from Medscape, September 2016, haemoglobin concentration._

**Causes**

Anaemia may result from decreased red blood cell production, increased red blood cell destruction, or loss of red blood cells.

**Decreased production of red blood cells**

- Nutritional iron deficiency
- Folic acid deficiency
- Vitamin B12 deficiency
- Depressed bone marrow function, such as leukaemia or aplasia
- Infections, such as HIV, TB, and visceral leishmaniasis
- Aplastic anaemia

**Increased destruction of red blood cells, or haemolysis**

- Malaria
- Medicine side effects, such as dapsone, cotrimoxazole, and AZT
- Congenital disorders, such as sickle cell anaemia and G6PD deficiency

**Loss of red blood cells**

- Acute or chronic blood loss, including:
    - Haemorrhage after trauma
    - Hookworm infestation
    - Pregnancy-related bleeding
    - Abortion
    - Heavy menstrual loss
    - Schistosomiasis
    - Massive or chronic gastrointestinal bleeding

**Clinical features**

Common clinical features include:

- Pallor of conjunctivae, mucous membranes, palms, and soles
- Fatigue
- Dizziness
- Palpitations
- Headache
- Anorexia
- Sometimes weight loss
- Low exercise tolerance

Features of severe anaemia may include signs of heart failure:

- Lower limb oedema
- Dyspnoea
- Tachycardia
- Heart murmurs

If anaemia is due to acute blood loss, features may include:

- Postural hypotension
- Decreased cardiac output
- Tachycardia
- Sweating
- Restlessness
- Thirst

Look for signs of specific pathology, such as:

- Splenomegaly
- Malaria
- Nutritional deficiency
- Haemolytic jaundice

**Investigations**

Initial investigations should include:

- Complete blood count with differentials
- Mean corpuscular volume
- Platelets
- Peripheral blood smear
- Reticulocyte count

Evaluate haemoglobin levels according to the patient’s age.

Classify anaemia according to mean corpuscular volume.

**Microcytic anaemia**

Microcytic anaemia means small red blood cells. Consider:

- Serum ferritin to evaluate iron deficiency
- Haemoglobin electrophoresis for thalassaemias
- Sideroblastic anaemia, which may be caused by medicines such as isoniazid and chloramphenicol
- Evaluation for chronic blood loss, especially gastrointestinal bleeding
- Stool analysis for parasites and occult blood

**Macrocytic anaemia**

Consider:

- Vitamin B12 levels
- Fasting serum folate levels
- TSH to evaluate thyroid disease
- Chronic alcohol abuse
- Medicine causes, such as zidovudine, methotrexate, hydroxyurea, and antifolate medicines

**Normocytic anaemia**

Consider:

- Acute blood loss
- Chronic disease
- Renal failure
- Haemolysis

Investigations for haemolysis may include:

- Peripheral blood film for schistocytes, suggesting microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia
- Haemoglobin studies where indicated

Do bone marrow aspirate and biopsy if bone marrow failure or haematological malignancy is suspected, such as:

- Aplastic anaemia
- Leukaemia
- Lymphoma
- Myeloma

> **Note**
> Anaemia is not a final diagnosis. Careful history, physical examination, and laboratory tests are essential to determine the cause.
>
**General principles of management**

- Determine and treat the cause.
- For example, antithymocyte globulin is used in aplastic anaemia and should be managed by a specialist.
- Consider the need for blood transfusion according to:
    - Haemoglobin level
    - Clinical condition
    - Haemodynamic status
    - Presence of heart failure
    - Ongoing blood loss

#### 11.1.1.1 Iron Deficiency Anaemia

**ICD-10 CODE:** D50

Iron deficiency anaemia is anaemia caused by inadequate iron availability, commonly due to poor intake, increased requirements, or chronic blood loss.

**Causes**

- Poor nutritional intake with iron-poor foods
- Chronic blood loss, including:
    - Hookworm infestation
    - Prolonged or excessive menstrual bleeding
    - Chronic gastrointestinal bleeding
    - Chronic use of NSAIDs
    - Large bowel tumours
    - Oesophageal varices

**Clinical features**

Iron deficiency anaemia usually develops slowly.

Features include general anaemia symptoms plus:

- Sore tongue
- Atrophy of lingual papillae
- Erosions at the corners of the mouth
- Brittle or fragile fingernails

**Differential diagnosis**

- Other conditions that cause microcytic red cells

**Investigations**

- Complete blood count
- Haemoglobin level
- Haematocrit, although haematocrit is rarely below 28% unless iron deficiency is present
- Low mean corpuscular volume
- Low mean corpuscular haemoglobin
- Hypochromia, which may not be obvious in patients who have already been transfused
- Hypochromic microcytic red cells
- Investigate the cause of iron deficiency

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Identify and treat the cause of iron deficiency. | HC2 |
| Adjust diet if poor diet is one of the underlying causes. | HC2 |
| Adult: give oral ferrous sulphate 200 mg every 8 hours. | HC2 |
| Alternatively, give ferrous sulphate / folic acid 200 mg / 0.4 mg every 8 hours. This is equivalent to 180 mg elemental iron per day. | HC2 |
| Child: give oral ferrous sulphate 5 mg/kg every 8 hours, maximum 200 mg per dose. This is equivalent to about 5 mg/kg elemental iron per day. | HC2 |
| Haemoglobin usually rises in 2 to 3 weeks and returns to normal after about 2 months. | HC2 |
| Continue treatment for 6 months to 1 year to replenish iron stores. | HC2 |
| Give an antihelminthic. Albendazole 400 mg as a single dose may be used. | HC2 |

Refer to hospital in case of:

- Severe symptoms requiring blood transfusion
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
- Malabsorption
- Intolerance to oral therapy
- Unclear cause
- Failure to improve

> **Note**
> Side effects of oral iron include diarrhoea, abdominal discomfort, constipation, and black stools. Warn the patient not to worry about black stools.
>
> Parenteral iron is rarely necessary and can cause anaphylaxis. It should only be used by specialists.
>
#### 11.1.1.2 Megaloblastic Anaemia

**ICD-10 CODE:** D51–D52

Megaloblastic anaemia is anaemia characterised by large red blood cells. It is usually due to folate deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, or both.

Some medicines, such as hydroxyurea, zidovudine, and stavudine, can cause macrocytic anaemia without folate or vitamin B12 deficiency.

**Causes**

- Low dietary intake of folate
- Increased folate requirements, such as in children and pregnancy
- Low dietary intake of vitamin B12, especially in exclusively vegetarian diets without animal proteins
- Malabsorption of folate and vitamin B12, such as in severe gastritis, giardia infection, or severe intestinal disease
- Medicines, including:
    - Metformin
    - Zidovudine
    - Hydroxyurea
    - Stavudine
    - Phenytoin
- Other causes of macrocytosis, including:
    - Myelodysplasia
    - Hypothyroidism
    - Chronic alcohol use
    - Multiple myeloma

**Clinical features**

- General features of anaemia
- Features of vitamin B12 deficiency, including:
    - Neuropsychiatric abnormalities
    - Hyperpigmented palms and feet
    - Smooth beefy tongue
    - Peripheral neuropathy
    - Impaired vibration and position sense
    - Abnormal gait
    - Weakness
    - Decreased muscle strength
    - Spastic movements
    - Memory loss
    - Disorientation
    - Depression
    - Acute confusional state

**Investigations**

- Blood smear showing macrocytosis
- Elevated mean corpuscular haemoglobin
- Elevated mean corpuscular volume
- Pancytopenia in severe cases
- Full blood count showing oval macrocytes
- Hypersegmentation of neutrophils
- Thrombocytopenia
- Decreased serum vitamin B12
- Decreased fasting red cell folate

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Identify and treat the underlying cause of anaemia. | HC2 / RR |
| Oral vitamin B12 may be given at a dose of 1 mg, equivalent to 1000 micrograms, daily in patients who cannot tolerate parenteral therapy. | HC2 / RR |
| A nasal formulation of vitamin B12 may be given on alternate days where available. | HC2 / RR |
| Advise dietary modification to ensure adequate intake of folate and vitamin B12. Encourage green leafy vegetables and foods of animal origin. | HC2 / RR |
| Give folic acid 5 mg daily until haemoglobin levels return to normal. | HC2 / RR |
| Give vitamin B12 1 mg IM daily for 5 days, then weekly for a further 3 doses. | HC2 / RR |
| In patients with pernicious anaemia, follow with vitamin B12 1 mg every second month for life. | HC2 / RR |

> **Caution**
> If vitamin B12 deficiency is suspected, such as with low leucocytes and platelets, neuropsychiatric symptoms, or vegan diet, do not give folic acid alone. Refer for further testing and treatment.
>
> Giving folic acid alone in patients with vitamin B12 deficiency may precipitate permanent neurological deficit.
>
> **Note**
> Anaemia normally corrects within 1 to 2 months. White cell count and thrombocytopenia usually normalise within 7 to 10 days.
>
> Do not use ferrous-folate combination tablets to treat folate deficiency because the quantity of folic acid is too low.
>
#### 11.1.1.3 Normocytic Anaemia

Normocytic anaemia is anaemia in which the red blood cells are of normal size, but the haemoglobin level is reduced.

**Causes**

- Haemolysis, meaning destruction of red blood cells, including:
    - Autoimmune disorders
    - Hypersplenism
    - Haemoglobin abnormalities, such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia
    - Medicine-related haemolysis, such as sulphonamides, dapsone, and primaquine
- Decreased reticulocytosis, meaning reduced formation of new red blood cells, including:
    - Chronic kidney disease
    - Chronic diseases

**Clinical features**

- General features of anaemia

**Investigations**

- Evidence of haemolysis
- Full blood count
- Peripheral blood smear, including assessment for spherocytes
- HIV serology

**Management**

| Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|
| Identify and treat the cause of anaemia. | HC4 |
| Do not treat with iron, folic acid, or vitamin B12 unless there is clear documented deficiency. | HC4 |
| Treat all patients with haemolytic anaemia with folic acid 5 mg daily. | HC4 |
| Refer to hospital for further management. | HC4 |

**Prevention and health education for anaemia**

Educate the public about:

- The lifelong effects of anaemia on health and cognitive development
- Dietary measures, including exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months
- Use of locally available iron-containing weaning foods, such as red meat, beans, peas, and dark leafy vegetables
- Hygiene measures, including avoiding walking barefoot to prevent hookworm infestation
- Use of pit latrines for faecal disposal
- Good handwashing practices
- Periodic screening for children and pregnant women
- Presumptive iron therapy for children and pregnant women in cases of anaemia, as guided by IMCI and pregnancy guidelines in chapters 16 and 17
- Routine iron supplementation for all pregnant women
- Early treatment of malaria, helminthic infections, and related causes


### 11.1.2 Bleeding Disorders

A bleeding disorder is suspected if a patient has unexplained bruising and bleeding, especially where there is no history of trauma. Prolonged bleeding or oozing may also occur after injury or surgery, such as tooth extraction or a small cut.

**Causes**

Bleeding disorders may result from blood vessel defects, platelet defects, or coagulation defects.

**Blood vessel defects**

- Acquired causes, including age and medicine side effects, such as steroids and NSAIDs
- Genetic causes, such as hereditary telangiectasia

**Platelet defects**

- Decreased platelet number or function, such as in:
    - Blood cancer
    - Viral infections
    - Aplastic anaemia
- Increased platelet destruction, such as in:
    - Hypersplenism
    - Autoimmune disease
    - Immune thrombocytopenic purpura
    - Massive blood transfusion

**Coagulation defects**

- Hereditary causes, such as haemophilia A, haemophilia B, and von Willebrand disease
- Acquired causes, such as:
    - Warfarin or heparin use
    - Liver disease
    - Alcoholism
    - Acquired factor inhibitors, for example in malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and pregnancy
- Infections such as meningococcal sepsis and haemorrhagic fevers, which may cause widespread endothelial damage and disseminated intravascular coagulation

**Clinical features**

Features suggesting platelet disorders include:

- Mucosal bleeding, such as gum bleeding or nose bleeds
- Superficial ecchymoses
- Excessive bleeding after minor injury
- Petechiae
- Heavy menstrual bleeding

Features suggesting coagulation disorders include:

- Large deep haematomas
- Haemarthrosis

**Investigations**

- Complete blood count
- Platelet count, which may be estimated using a peripheral smear if an auto-analyser is not available
- Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy if haematological malignancy is suspected as the cause of bleeding
- Bleeding time
- Coagulation tests

Bleeding time is normal with coagulation factor deficiencies, except von Willebrand disease. It is abnormal in thrombocytopenia and qualitative platelet defects.

Coagulation tests include:

- Prothrombin time, which is prolonged in factor VII, X, V, and II deficiencies, liver disease, and warfarin treatment
- International normalised ratio, used to monitor anticoagulation with warfarin
- Partial thromboplastin time, which is prolonged in factor VIII, XII, XI, IX, X, V, and I deficiencies

> **Note**
> INR is not useful for monitoring heparin or direct-acting anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban.
>
If the bleeding disorder is acute, consider haemorrhagic fevers as a possible cause.

**Management**

Patients with acute bleeding disorders should be referred to hospital for appropriate investigations and treatment.

Patients with chronic bleeding disorders should be referred to a specialist.

| Clinical situation | Treatment | LOC |
|---|---|---|
| General management | Identify and treat the root cause of the bleeding disorder. | HC2 / HC4 |
| Newborn requiring vitamin K | Give phytomenadione, or vitamin K, injection. Full-term newborn: 1 mg IM or IV. Preterm newborn: 500 micrograms IM or IV. Repeat every 8 hours if necessary. | HC2 / HC4 |
| Patient on warfarin with acute bleeding | Give vitamin K 5 mg slow IV to reverse warfarin effect. | HC4 |
| Severe or active bleeding in a patient on warfarin | Give fresh frozen plasma. | HC4 |
| Medicines interfering with coagulation or platelet function | Discontinue medicines that interfere with coagulation or platelet function, such as cephalosporins, dipyridazole, thiazides, alcohol, chlorpromazine, sulfonamides, rifampicin, methyldopa, phenytoin, barbiturates, quinidine, and isoniazid. | HC4 |
| Immune thrombocytopenic purpura | First-line treatment is oral prednisolone 0.5 to 2 mg/kg/day. | H |
| Immune thrombocytopenic purpura requiring second-line treatment | Rituximab and intravenous immunoglobulin are second-line treatments and should be given by a specialist. | H |
| Platelet transfusion | Transfuse platelets if the patient is bleeding, or prophylactically when platelet count is less than 10,000/µL in patients at high risk of bleeding, such as cancer patients. | H |
| Clotting factor-related bleeding | Transfuse fresh frozen plasma if bleeding is thought to be due to clotting factor disorders. | H |
| If above options are not viable | Refer to a higher level of care. | RR |

**Referral criteria**

Refer the patient to hospital if any of the following are present:

- Cause cannot be determined locally
- Spontaneous bleeding
- Bleeding into muscles or joints
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
- Central nervous system bleeding
- Bleeding in patients on warfarin
- Postpartum bleeding
- Family history of bleeding

**Health education**

Advise patients with chronic bleeding disorders to:

- Prevent injury
- Avoid injections and unnecessary surgery
- Visit the clinic immediately if symptoms occur
- Continue all medicines as prescribed

All haemophiliacs should receive prophylactic treatment before traumatic procedures, such as tooth extraction or surgery.

### 11.1.3 Sickle Cell Disease

**ICD-10 CODE:** D57

Sickle cell disease is an inherited disorder in which red blood cells that carry oxygen around the body change shape from a smooth doughnut shape into a crescent or half-moon shape. It is sometimes called sickle cell anaemia.

**Cause**

Sickle cell disease is caused by a defect in the beta chains of haemoglobin where valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 of the chain. This change creates abnormal haemoglobin called HbS.

**Clinical features**

Symptoms usually appear from 3 to 6 months of age and may include:

- Anaemia
- Dactylitis, or swelling of fingers
- Lobar pneumonia
- Recurrent severe bacterial infections

These features result from reduction of foetal haemoglobin, HbF, and increase in HbS in the blood.

**Chronic anaemia**

Chronic anaemia is common, usually with haemoglobin of 6 to 9 g/dL. Episodes of acute worsening may occur due to:

- Aplastic crisis, which is sudden transient arrest of blood cell production in the bone marrow, with low haemoglobin and low reticulocytes, often due to parvovirus B19 infection
- Splenic sequestration, which is pooling of large amounts of red blood cells in the spleen, with painful rapidly enlarging spleen, decreasing haemoglobin, and high reticulocyte count

**Acute vaso-occlusive phenomena**

Vaso-occlusion may cause:

- Painful crisis, with acute intense pain in the back, chest, limbs, or abdomen
- Pain and swelling of hands and feet in children below 2 years
- Stroke, with hemiplegia, altered consciousness, or seizures
- Acute chest syndrome, with fever, chest pain, difficulty in breathing, low oxygen level, cough, or wheezing
- Acute abdomen or mesenteric crisis, with abdominal pain and distension, reduced or absent bowel sounds, pallor, and fever
- Renal infarction
- Bone infarction and necrosis, especially at the head of the femur
- Priapism

In acute abdomen or mesenteric crisis, abdominal X-ray may show dilated bowel loops. Anaemia, high reticulocyte count, and high CRP may be present.

**Chronic organ damage**

Chronic organ damage may result from anaemia and vaso-occlusive phenomena. It may include:

- Hyposplenia or autosplenectomy due to multiple splenic infarcts
- Pulmonary hypertension
- Asthma
- Chronic renal disease
- Chronic hepatic disease
- Gallbladder stones
- Nephropathy
- Acute kidney injury due to hypovolaemia and hypoperfusion
- Osteoporosis
- Retinopathy
- Chronic leg ulcers

Transcranial Doppler velocity above 200 cm/second predicts high risk of stroke. This is less predictive in adults.

Patients with asplenia or hyposplenism are at increased risk of infections such as:

- Pneumococcal infections
- Osteomyelitis
- Pneumonia
- Septicaemia

**Investigations**

- Family history of sickle cell disease
- Full blood count
- Peripheral film comment
- Screening tests for sickling, although these are not fully reliable
- Haemoglobin electrophoresis to confirm diagnosis
- Chest radiography for acute chest syndrome
- Abdominal ultrasound
- Urinalysis
- Liver function tests
- Renal function tests

**Chronic management**

**Hydroxyurea**

Hydroxyurea may be used under specialist management.

Starting dose:

- Hydroxyurea 20 mg/kg

Children aged 9 months and above should be initiated on hydroxyurea when indicated.

Indications for hydroxyurea include:

- Frequent crises, meaning more than 3 crises in a year
- Pain interfering with activities of daily living
- Abnormal transcranial Doppler ultrasonography velocity above 200 cm/second
- Recurrent or severe acute chest syndrome
- Stroke

> **Note**
> The decision to give hydroxyurea should be made by a senior health worker after full laboratory investigation, including complete blood count, renal function tests, and liver function tests.
>

**Prevention and health education**

- Provide patient, family, and community education.
- Ensure timely initiation of hydroxyurea where indicated.
- Conduct periodic comprehensive evaluations and other disease-specific health maintenance services.
- Periodically evaluate for sickle cell complications, such as:
    - Urinalysis and renal function tests for sickle cell nephropathy
    - Echocardiography for pulmonary hypertension
    - Transcranial Doppler in children for early detection of stroke risk
- Refer patients with complications to a specialist.
- Ensure timely and appropriate treatment of painful crises and acute illness.
- Provide genetic counselling for couples planning to have children.
- Conduct antenatal screening.
- Support early recognition and screening of children with low haemoglobin.
- Ensure vaccination, including pneumococcal vaccine, *Haemophilus influenzae* vaccine, and hepatitis B vaccine.
- Provide antibiotic prophylaxis, such as oral penicillin twice daily in eligible children.
- Provide antimalarial chemoprophylaxis where indicated.


## 11.2 Blood and Blood Products

The Uganda Blood Transfusion Service collects blood and produces all blood products.

Whole blood is unseparated blood collected in an approved container and containing a preservative or anticoagulant solution.

In this guideline, “blood” refers to any blood component in which the main constituent is red blood cells, such as:

- Whole blood

- Red cell concentrate

- Red cell suspension

Unless otherwise specified, other components are referred to as blood components or products. Blood components are prepared from whole blood and contain negligible quantities of red cells, such as:

- Platelet concentrate

- Fresh frozen plasma

- Cryoprecipitate

> **Note**
>
> Refer to the National Blood Transfusion Guidelines for the appropriate use of blood.
>
The Uganda Blood Transfusion Service ensures that all blood and blood products are produced in a way that protects the health and safety of both patients and donors and minimises the risk of transmitting infection through blood.

### 11.2.1 General Principles of Good Clinical Practice in Transfusion Medicine

Blood is a scarce and expensive resource. Blood transfusion carries risks of adverse reactions and transfusion-transmitted infections.

Use blood appropriately, meaning only to treat conditions that can lead to significant morbidity or mortality and cannot be prevented or effectively managed by other means.

**Minimise the need for transfusion by:**

- Early diagnosis and treatment of anaemia, particularly iron deficiency anaemia
- Stopping blood loss through good surgical and anaesthetic management
- Appropriate and timely management of coagulation disorders
- Use of simple alternatives to transfusion when appropriate, such as IV fluids as first-line treatment for hypovolaemic shock

Prescribe transfusion according to the patient’s individual needs, using clinical signs and symptoms and the expected outcome. Do not prescribe transfusion based only on haemoglobin level.

**Do not use blood transfusion to:**

- Expand blood volume, unless there has been blood loss of more than 30% of total blood volume
- Enhance wound healing
- “Top up” haemoglobin before surgery
- Improve general well-being of the patient
- Replace ongoing fluid losses, such as surgical blood loss, without clear clinical indication

Blood should not be transfused unless it has been:

- Obtained from appropriately selected donors, preferably voluntary non-remunerated donors
- Screened for transfusion-transmissible infections, namely:
    - HIV
    - Hepatitis B
    - Hepatitis C
    - Syphilis
- Tested for compatibility between the donor’s red cells and the antibodies in the patient’s plasma, in accordance with national guidelines

The mandate to collect blood from donors and screen it for transfusion-transmissible infections is reserved for the Uganda Blood Transfusion Service.

Guidelines and procedures for requesting, administering, and recording blood transfusion should be clearly spelled out and strictly followed to avoid catastrophic mistakes.

Ensure that the transfused patient is closely monitored during and after transfusion, and that there is immediate response if any adverse reaction occurs.

### 11.2.2 Blood and Blood Products: Characteristics and Indications

The following section presents only whole blood and red cell concentrate. Availability and use of other blood components is reserved for referral hospitals and is beyond the scope of this guideline.

#### 11.2.2.1 Whole Blood

Whole blood provides:

- Red blood cells
- Plasma volume
- Stable coagulation factors, including factors VII and XI
- Other blood components

Whole blood may not have enough functional platelets and labile coagulation factors, including factors V and VIII.

Whole blood is also used as raw material from which other blood components are prepared.

One unit of whole blood is about 450 mL of donor blood obtained from a single donation, plus 63 mL of anticoagulant or preservative solution. It is available from HC4 level.

The haematocrit is approximately 35%.

Each unit of blood will raise haemoglobin by about 1 g/dL.

**Indications**

- Red blood cell replacement in acute blood loss with significant hypovolaemia, such as:
    - Trauma
    - Surgery
    - Invasive procedures
    - Gastrointestinal haemorrhage
- Patients who need red blood cell transfusion where red cell concentrates or suspensions are not available
- Exchange transfusion in neonates, using blood units less than 5 days old, for specialist use only

Consider adding furosemide where there is risk of fluid overload.

**Dose**

- Adults: 1 unit at a time
- Children: 20 mL/kg

> **Caution**
> Transfusion must be started within 30 minutes of removal from the refrigerator and completed within 4 hours of starting.
>
> Whole blood should be stored at 2–6°C in an approved blood bank refrigerator with temperature charts and alarm.
>
> Whole blood is contraindicated in severe chronic anaemia and incipient cardiac failure because of the risk of volume overload.
>
> Blood should not be warmed using improvised warming methods commonly used in health facilities. This is not necessary.
>
> Routine use of diuretics such as furosemide before transfusion is not necessary in most patients. Pre-transfusion diuretics are indicated in known cardiac and renal patients to prevent circulatory overload.
>
#### 11.2.2.2 Red Cell Concentrates / Packed Red Cells

Red cell concentrates, also called packed red cells, contain 80 to 150 mL per unit and are obtained from a single donation.

The haematocrit is approximately 55%.

Red cell concentrates are available from HC4 level.

**Indications**

- Red blood cell replacement in anaemic patients
- Acute blood loss, together with crystalloid solution if whole blood is not available

> **Caution**
> Transfusion must be started within 30 minutes of removal from the refrigerator and completed within 4 hours of starting.
>
> Red cell concentrates should be stored at 2–6°C in an approved blood bank refrigerator with temperature charts and alarm.
>
#### 11.2.2.3 Clinical Indications for Blood Transfusion

The indication for blood transfusion, using whole blood or red cell concentrates, depends on:

- Degree of anaemia, estimated by haemoglobin level
- Clinical condition, including high risk or presence of signs and symptoms of tissue hypoxia
- Impaired tissue oxygenation resulting from anaemia or blood loss
- Presence of ongoing blood loss, such as internal or external haemorrhage

**Severe acute anaemia in children and infants**

Transfuse if:

- Haemoglobin is ≤4 g/dL, or haematocrit is ≤12%, regardless of clinical condition
- Haemoglobin is 4–6 g/dL, or haematocrit is 13–18%, with life-threatening complications such as:
    - Clinical features of hypoxia
    - Cardiac decompensation
    - Acidosis, usually causing respiratory distress
    - Impaired consciousness or coma
    - Hyperparasitaemia above 20%
    - Cerebral malaria
    - Septicaemia
    - Meningitis

**Dose**

- Packed red cells: 10–15 mL/kg
- Whole blood: 20 mL/kg

> **Note**
> In children with chronic anaemia caused by iron deficiency, it may be possible to correct the anaemia with iron therapy alone.
>
**Severe anaemia in adults**

Consider blood transfusion only where the severity of anaemia is likely to cause, or has already caused, clinical signs of hypoxia or impaired tissue oxygenation.

Signs may include:

- Tachycardia
- Shock
- Respiratory distress
- Weakness
- Dizziness
- Unconsciousness

Transfusion may be indicated in:

- Symptomatic anaemia in adults with haemoglobin below 7 g/dL
- Haemoglobin below 8 g/dL in patients with cardiac disease or central nervous system symptoms

Give the minimum number of transfusions necessary to relieve hypoxia:

- Transfuse 1 unit at a time, then reassess
- If symptoms persist, give another unit
- Transfuse over 2 to 4 hours

**Severe anaemia in pregnancy**

Screen for iron deficiency anaemia early in pregnancy and treat with iron where necessary.

**Pregnancy below 36 weeks**

Transfuse if:

- Haemoglobin is 5 g/dL, irrespective of clinical condition
- Haemoglobin is 5–7 g/dL with established or incipient heart failure, impaired tissue oxygenation, pneumonia, other serious infection, malaria, or pre-existing heart disease

**Pregnancy above 36 weeks**

Transfuse if:

- Haemoglobin is ≤6 g/dL
- Haemoglobin is 6–8 g/dL with established or incipient heart failure, impaired tissue oxygenation, pneumonia, other serious infection, malaria, or pre-existing heart disease

**Elective caesarean section**

If there is a history of antepartum haemorrhage, postpartum haemorrhage, or previous caesarean section:

- If haemoglobin is 8–10 g/dL, establish or confirm blood group and save freshly taken serum for cross-matching
- If haemoglobin is below 8 g/dL, have 2 units of blood cross-matched and made available

**Pre-operative anaemia**

Pre-operative anaemia should be investigated and promptly managed before surgery.

Prompt management may include iron supplementation, either oral or intravenous.

Where possible, surgery should be delayed or postponed until anaemia is corrected, because pre-operative anaemia is associated with poor surgical outcomes and increased need for blood transfusion.

Blood transfusion may be considered at haemoglobin ≤8 g/dL in case of:

- Inadequate compensation for anaemia, meaning symptomatic anaemia
- Significant co-existing cardiorespiratory disease
- Major surgery or expected significant blood loss
- Failure to correct anaemia before surgery

**Management of acute haemorrhage / hypovolaemia**

IV fluids, especially crystalloids such as normal saline, are first-line treatment for hypovolaemia during acute haemorrhage.

Whole blood, or red blood cells if whole blood is unavailable, are indicated when blood loss is more than 20–30% of blood volume, equivalent to more than 15–20 mL/kg.

The need for blood must be determined by:

- Amount and speed of blood loss
- Patient’s critical signs
- Initial response to IV fluid resuscitation

> **Note**
> Haemoglobin level is not a reliable indicator of blood need in acute haemorrhage.
>
**Sickle cell anaemia**

Blood transfusion is not necessary for an asymptomatic sickle cell patient with steady haemoglobin of 6–8 g/dL, or for an uncomplicated painful episode.

In addition to general indications, blood transfusion is indicated if:

- Acute severe anaemia occurs, with haemoglobin below 5 g/dL or 2 g/dL lower than the patient’s usual level, such as in aplastic crisis or acute sequestration crisis
- Haemoglobin is below 6 g/dL in uncomplicated pregnancy
- Haemoglobin is below 8 g/dL if caesarean section is planned
- Haemoglobin is below 9 g/dL in acute chest syndrome or stroke

For pregnancy, caesarean section, acute chest syndrome, and stroke, the target haemoglobin is 11 g/dL and not higher.

Use packed cells if available rather than whole blood.

**Neonatal conditions**

Transfusion may be indicated in severe anaemia of any cause, including prematurity and sepsis.

Transfusion in neonates should be managed at specialist level.


### 11.2.3 Adverse Reactions Following Transfusion

Adverse reactions following transfusion may be acute or delayed.

**Acute transfusion reactions may include:**

- Minor allergic reaction, such as urticaria
- Febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reaction
- Acute haemolytic transfusion reaction, commonly caused by ABO incompatibility, which is severe and life-threatening
- Bacterial contamination
- Transfusion-associated circulatory overload
- Transfusion-related acute lung injury
- Severe allergic reaction, including anaphylaxis, which is relatively rare

**Delayed transfusion reactions may include:**

- Transfusion-transmitted infections, such as HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C
- Delayed haemolytic transfusion reactions

**General principles**

- Acute transfusion reactions may occur in 1 to 2% of transfused patients.
- Rapid recognition and management of transfusion reactions may save the patient’s life.
- Accurate patient identification at the bedside is critical during:
    - Blood sample collection
    - Administration of blood
- Monitoring transfusion is the only way to identify acute transfusion reactions.
- Monitoring is performed by taking vital signs:
    - Before transfusion
    - 15 minutes after starting transfusion
    - Whenever a reaction is suspected
    - At the end of transfusion
- Vital signs should always be taken at minimum:
    - Immediately before beginning the transfusion
    - 15 minutes after starting the transfusion
    - At the end of the transfusion
- A nurse or physician should observe the patient during the first 15 minutes after a new blood unit is started, and vital signs should be recorded.
- Errors and failure to follow correct procedures are the most common causes of life-threatening acute haemolytic reactions.

Common errors include:

- Misidentification of patients
- Administering the wrong blood unit to the wrong patient
- Failure to repeat blood grouping of blood units received at the hospital
- Failure to cross-match
- Errors in labelling blood samples for pre-transfusion grouping and cross-match

These errors must be avoided.

Always store blood used for compatibility testing for 7 days at 2 to 8°C for possible investigation of transfusion reactions.

In a conscious patient with a severe acute haemolytic transfusion reaction, signs and symptoms may appear within minutes after infusion of only 5 to 10 mL of blood.

In an unconscious or anaesthetised patient, hypotension, hypoxia, and uncontrolled bleeding may be the only signs of a transfusion problem. Regular monitoring of vital signs is therefore important.

**Vital signs to monitor**

- Temperature
- Blood pressure
- Respiratory rate
- Pulse rate

Any unexpected change in vital signs should be treated as a possible acute transfusion reaction until proved otherwise.

**If a transfusion reaction is suspected**

- Stop the transfusion and remove the giving set.
- Before disconnecting, close the blood unit to avoid reflux of patient blood into the donor blood.
- Check the blood pack labels and the patient’s identity.
- If there is any discrepancy, consult the blood bank.
- Evaluate the patient, take vital signs, and manage accordingly.
- Maintain intravenous access.
- Obtain a post-transfusion blood sample.
- Return the implicated blood unit to the hospital blood bank.
- Re-grouping and testing should be done on both patient and transfused samples.
- Immediately report all suspected acute transfusion reactions to the hospital blood bank laboratory working with the clinician.
- For severe reactions, record the following in the patient’s notes:
    - Type of reaction
    - Time the reaction occurred from the start of transfusion
    - Volume transfused
    - Type of blood product transfused
    - Pack numbers of blood products transfused
- Diagnose the type of reaction and start a quick, clear investigation in the hospital blood bank laboratory.

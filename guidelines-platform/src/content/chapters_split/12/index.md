# Chapter 12: Oncology

Cancer is the unregulated growth of previously normal body cells. Oncology is the study, diagnosis, and management of cancers or tumours.

Any organ or body system can be affected by cancer, and cancer can affect any individual. This chapter outlines major symptoms and signs of cancer, key population groups at increased risk, ways to reduce cancer risk, and an overview of common cancers in adults and children.

Cancer, or malignant neoplasm, is a collective term for a group of more than 100 diseases that result from abnormal or uncontrolled growth of body cells. Cancer can invade normal tissues and spread to other parts of the body.

The uncontrolled growth may cause a lump or swelling called a tumour or neoplasm in many types of cancer. In some cancers, such as blood cancers including leukaemia, there may be an abnormal number of abnormal cells.

Tumours are broadly divided into:

- Benign tumours, meaning non-cancerous tumours that are unable to metastasise or spread to other parts of the body
- Malignant tumours, meaning cancerous tumours that can invade normal tissues or spread to other parts of the body

Cancers are classified by their type of cell, tissue, or organ of origin.

In Uganda, it was estimated that in 2020 there were:

- 34,008 new cancer cases
- 22,992 cancer deaths
- 62,548 adults living with cancer

The top five causes of cancer morbidity in Uganda are:

- Cervical cancer
- Kaposi sarcoma
- Breast cancer
- Prostate cancer
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma

The top five causes of cancer deaths in Uganda are:

- Cervical cancer
- Kaposi sarcoma
- Oesophageal cancer
- Liver cancer
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma

Children aged 0 to 14 years constitute about 10% of cancer patients in Uganda.

## 12.1 Cancer Recognition and Risk

### 12.1.1 Special Groups at Increased Risk of Cancer

Special groups at increased risk of cancer include:

- HIV-positive patients
- People with albinism
- People aged above 65 years
- Women, especially for breast and cervical cancer
- Smokers
- People who consume alcohol heavily
- People with consistent occupational exposure to toxins or radioactive material

> **Note**
> Routine screening is recommended for these groups.
>
### 12.1.2 Early Signs and Symptoms

Early signs and symptoms that may suggest cancer include:

- Sudden weight loss
- Painless or painful swelling, lump, or thickening
- Sores that fail to heal
- Hoarseness or cough
- Abnormal bleeding or discharge
- Persistent indigestion or difficulty in swallowing
- Change in normal bowel or bladder habits
- Chronic ulcers
- Chronic pain
- Change in a skin wart or mole

#### 12.1.2.1 Urgent Signs and Symptoms

Each type of cancer may present with unique signs and symptoms. However, many cancer patients report having experienced or noticed some symptoms or signs for weeks, months, or years before they became very sick.

Cancer should be investigated in an individual with the following common signs and symptoms, especially when they have occurred for more than 2 weeks:

- Sudden unexplained weight loss
- Painless or painful swelling, lump, or thickening
- Sores that fail to heal
- Hoarseness or cough
- Abnormal bleeding or discharge
- Persistent indigestion or difficulty in swallowing
- Change in normal bowel or bladder habits
- Chronic ulcers
- Chronic pain
- Change in a skin wart or mole

Urgent referral for possible cancer or malignancy may be necessary in patients with any of the following:

| Body part or system | Signs and symptoms |
|---|---|
| Haematological | Neutropenia, anaemia, infection, bleeding, hyperviscosity, leukocytosis greater than 50 × 10⁶ |
| Lung, excluding TB | Coughing blood, superior vena cava obstruction |
| Upper gastrointestinal tract | Chronic gastrointestinal bleeding and bowel habit changes, dysphagia, persistent vomiting, unexplained pain and weight loss, abdominal mass without dyspepsia, obstructive jaundice |
| Lower gastrointestinal tract | Bleeding and bowel habit changes, palpable rectal mass, unexplained iron deficiency anaemia |
| Breast | Discrete hard lump with fixation, eczematous skin and nipple changes, unilateral nipple discharge |
| Gynaecological | Postmenopausal bleeding, persistent intermenstrual bleeding, vulval lump and bleeding |
| Urological | Hard irregular prostate, urinary symptoms, macroscopic haematuria, swelling or mass in testes, or any abdominal mass along the urological tract |
| Central nervous system | Progressive neurological deficit, new-onset seizures, headaches, mental changes, unilateral deafness, and signs of raised intracranial pressure such as vomiting, drowsiness, posture-related headache, tinnitus, and other CNS symptoms |

## 12.2 Prevention of Cancer

Cancer prevention means activities or actions directed at avoiding, reducing, eliminating, or eradicating the risk of developing cancer or reducing the impact of cancer on individuals and populations.

Approximately 40% of cancers are preventable through interventions such as:

- Prevention of oncogenic infections, including HPV, HIV, and hepatitis B virus
- Reduction of alcohol use
- Tobacco control
- Environmental controls
- Promotion of healthy diets
- Physical activity

> **Note**
> Prevention offers the most cost-effective long-term strategy for cancer control.
>
Health workers are responsible for educating the public on:

- Primary prevention, which involves sustained action to prevent a cancerous process from developing through risk factor reduction
- Secondary prevention, which involves active discovery and control of cancerous or pre-cancerous lesions

### 12.2.1 Primary Prevention

Prevention of cancer includes activities or actions directed at avoiding, reducing, eliminating, or eradicating the risk of developing cancer before the onset of cancer.

Primary prevention gives individuals control in maintaining a healthy lifestyle and environment to avoid or reduce cancer risk.

#### 12.2.1.1 Control of Risk Factors

**Smoking and tobacco use**

Tobacco use increases the risk of several types of cancer, especially cancers of the:

- Lungs
- Oesophagus
- Larynx
- Mouth
- Throat
- Kidney
- Bladder
- Pancreas
- Stomach
- Cervix

Health workers must educate patients, clients, and communities on the dangers of tobacco consumption and smoking. Patients should be advised to avoid tobacco use. Patients or clients who smoke or use tobacco in any other form should be encouraged and supported to stop tobacco use.

**Unhealthy diet**

Consumption of an unhealthy diet, including unbalanced diets, sweetened foods and beverages, charred food, and unhygienic food, increases the risk of several types of cancer, especially cancers of the:

- Colon and rectum
- Mouth
- Pharynx
- Larynx
- Corpus uteri
- Breast
- Kidney
- Liver
- Pancreas
- Oesophagus
- Thyroid
- Prostate
- Multiple myeloma
- Gallbladder

Health workers must educate patients, clients, and communities to:

- Balance their diet with different types of healthy foods.
- Eat plenty of healthy foods such as whole grains, pulses, fruits, and vegetables.
- Limit foods that are high in sugar or fat.
- Avoid sugary drinks.
- Limit salt intake.
- Limit red meat.
- Avoid processed meat.
- Avoid burnt or charred food.

**Overweight and obesity**

Being overweight or obese increases the risk of cancer, especially cancers of the:

- Oesophagus
- Colon and rectum
- Breast
- Endometrium
- Kidney

Health workers must advise patients to maintain a healthy lifestyle, especially through regular physical activity and a healthy diet.

Patients should also be advised to maintain their body weight within the healthy range.

**Physical inactivity**

A sedentary lifestyle increases the risk of several cancers, including:

- Colon cancer
- Endometrial cancer
- Bladder cancer
- Breast cancer
- Lung cancer
- Oesophageal adenocarcinoma
- Renal cancer
- Gastric cancer

Health workers must advise patients and clients to be physically active in everyday life. They should limit the time spent sitting and engage in at least 30 minutes of regular physical activity per day or on most days of the week.

**Alcohol use**

Excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of cancer of the:

- Oral cavity
- Oesophagus
- Larynx
- Liver
- Colon and rectum
- Breast

Health workers should educate patients, clients, and communities on the dangers of excessive and regular alcohol consumption.

Key messages should include:

- Not drinking alcohol is better for cancer prevention.
- If a person drinks alcohol of any type, they should limit intake.

**Environmental pollution**

Regular exposure to carcinogenic chemicals in the environment can occur through:

- Unsafe drinking water
- Air pollution
- Food contaminated by aflatoxin or dioxin chemicals
- Occupational exposure to dangerous gases or dusts

Environmental carcinogens include:

- Aflatoxins
- Asbestos
- Vehicle emissions
- Lead
- Ultraviolet light
- Ionising radiation

Exposure to environmental carcinogens increases the risk of developing cancer, for example lung cancer.

Health workers must educate patients on environmental dangers and provide advice to limit exposure, including:

- Limiting indoor air pollution caused by smoke from charcoal and firewood used inside poorly ventilated houses
- Avoiding fumes from cars
- Avoiding exposure to garbage pollution, including burning rubbish
- Encouraging employers to provide employees with a safe working environment with limited occupational hazards

**Oncogenic infections**

The following infections are associated with certain types of cancer:

- Viral hepatitis B and C: liver cancer
- Human papillomavirus: cervical, oral, and anal cancers
- *Helicobacter pylori*: gastric cancer
- HIV/AIDS: aggressive lymphoma subtypes, Kaposi sarcoma, anorectal cancer, cervical cancer, and other cancers
- Schistosomiasis: increased risk of bladder cancer
- Liver fluke: increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma

Preventive measures to control oncogenic infection risk include vaccination, prevention of infection, and treatment of infection or infestation.

Health workers should advise patients and communities to:

- Engage in safe sexual behaviour to avoid sexually transmitted infections that can cause or increase the risk of cancers such as cervical cancer, Kaposi sarcoma, lymphoma, and liver cancer.
- Vaccinate all girls aged 10 years with 2 doses of HPV vaccine. See section 18 on immunisation.
- Ensure hepatitis B vaccination, which is routinely offered in the national childhood immunisation schedule and to populations at risk. Hepatitis B vaccination helps prevent hepatitis B infection, the main risk factor for liver cancer. See section 18 on immunisation.
- Treat HIV/AIDS, schistosomiasis, *H. pylori*, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and other infections as part of cancer prevention.

**Radiation**

Ultraviolet radiation, particularly solar radiation, is carcinogenic to humans. It causes major types of skin cancer, including:

- Basal cell carcinoma
- Squamous cell carcinoma
- Melanoma

People with albinism are at much higher risk of skin cancer. Health workers should encourage them to wear protective clothing and wide-brimmed hats.

Ionising radiation from radioactive isotopes used in medical diagnostics and treatment is associated with leukaemia and other solid tissue tumours. Proper disposal of highly radioactive isotopes is mandatory to prevent hazardous exposure.

### 12.2.2 Secondary Prevention

Secondary prevention of cancer includes activities or actions directed at halting the progress of cancer at its early stage through screening, early diagnosis, pre-cancer treatment, cancer management, and referral to avoid or reduce complications.

Secondary prevention strategies relate to the discovery and control of cancerous or pre-cancerous lesions.

Early detection of cancer greatly increases the chances of successful treatment and cure. It includes:

- Early diagnosis in symptomatic populations
- Screening in asymptomatic high-risk populations

Screening refers to the use of simple tests across a healthy population to identify individuals who have disease but do not yet have symptoms.

Based on existing evidence, mass population screening is advocated for breast and cervical cancer. Other cancers commonly screened for include prostate and colorectal cancers.

**Screening for breast cancer**

Screening or health check-up for breast cancer involves breast self-examination, clinical breast examination, mammography, and breast ultrasound where indicated.

**Breast self-examination**

Breast self-examination is a simple and quick examination done by the client herself, aimed at early detection of lumps.

Regular monthly breast self-examination is important. It should not be done during menstruation, but at least 7 days after menstruation ends.

Correct technique is important and is easy to teach and administer.

Health workers should note that breast self-examination is not a standard screening test for breast cancer, but it is beneficial for breast health awareness.

**Clinical breast examination**

Clinical breast examination is performed by a trained and skilled health care provider from HC3 level.

During clinical breast examination:

- Take a detailed history.
- Conduct a physical examination.
- Examine all breast quadrants in detail.
- Examine the armpits for lymph nodes.
- Inspect the skin for changes and swellings.
- Inspect for tethering of the breast on the chest wall.
- Palpate for lumps.
- Check for nipple discharge.

A suspicious lump or bloody nipple discharge must be referred for evaluation by mammography or ultrasonography, as well as core needle biopsy.

**Mammography**

Mammography is a low-dose X-ray of the breast. It is the test of choice for screening for early breast cancer, but it is available only at national referral hospital level.

**Breast ultrasound**

Breast ultrasound is not used as a routine screening test, but it is useful as an additional tool in characterising palpable tumours and taking image-directed biopsies.

It may be used as a screening tool in:

- Lactating women
- Women with small breasts
- Males

It may also be used as a diagnostic test in symptomatic patients.

**Screening for cervical cancer**

Screening for cervical cancer aims to detect pre-cancerous lesions, which are then treated to prevent progression to invasive cancer.

Recommended screening methods include:

- Visual inspection with acetic acid
- Visual inspection with Lugol’s iodine
- Cytology testing by Pap smear
- HPV DNA testing

**Visual inspection with acetic acid**

Visual inspection with acetic acid involves applying 3 to 5% freshly prepared acetic acid to the cervix and observing results after 1 minute.

VIA results are generally categorised into 3 groups:

- Suspicious for cancer
- VIA negative
- VIA positive

VIA uses readily available equipment, does not require a laboratory, and provides an immediate result.

Positive cases can be treated with cryotherapy by adequately trained providers.

**Considerations when using VIA as a screening method**

- Women below 25 years should be screened only if they are at high risk for disease, such as HIV positive women, women with early sexual exposure, multiple partners, previous abnormal screening results, or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia.
- VIA is not appropriate for women above 50 years.
- Screening is advised every 3 to 5 years if results are normal.
- Screening is advised after 1 year if results are abnormal and treatment such as cryotherapy has been provided.
- Screening is advised every year in HIV positive women.

**Visual inspection with Lugol’s iodine**

Visual inspection with Lugol’s iodine involves looking at the cervix with the naked eye or low magnification after swabbing with Lugol’s iodine.

VILI has a sensitivity of about 92% and specificity of about 85%.

Test results are available immediately, which reduces loss to follow-up.

The recommendations and timings outlined for VIA also apply to VILI.

**Cytology testing by Pap smear**

Pap smear is a microscopic examination of cells scraped from the opening of the cervix.

The Pap smear is best taken around mid-cycle.

It should be postponed in case of cervicitis until after treatment because pus cells obscure the clarity of the smear and affect interpretation.

Pap smear requires histocytology services and is therefore available only at referral facilities.

**HPV DNA testing**

HPV DNA testing is currently being piloted as a standard screening test in Uganda.
